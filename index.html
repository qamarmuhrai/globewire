Tibet Earthquake Toll Confirmed: 16 Dead, 32 Injured and 339 Homes Collapsed in Nagqu

At least 16 people were killed and 32 injured when a magnitude 7 earthquake struck China's Tibet region late on Thursday, state news agency Xinhua reported, citing Nagqu city's emergency management bureau. Eleven of the injured are in serious condition. The Nagqu media office said at least 339 homes collapsed, and police and fire rescue teams were erecting tents to shelter people displaced by the shaking. The quake hit at 10:33 p.m. local time at a shallow depth — put at 10 kilometres by China's Earthquake Networks Centre and 18 kilometres by the US Geological Survey — with its epicentre in a sparsely populated, high-altitude area about 190 kilometres east-northeast of Nagqu and roughly 390 kilometres from the regional capital, Lhasa. Houses in villages nearest the epicentre showed cracks, and footage released by official media showed residents leaving their homes to shelter outside or in cars. China's Earthquake Administration activated a Level II emergency response. Broadcaster CCTV described it as the biggest earthquake in the region in five years, and the strongest recorded in China since January 2024.

Global Tech Rout: Nasdaq Suffers Worst Day Since June as AI Spending Fears and the PHP Security Scare Hit Markets

Stock markets fell sharply on Thursday as investors dumped technology shares and sought safety. The Nasdaq dropped about 2.6 percent — its steepest one-day fall since June — shedding roughly $1 trillion in market value, while the S&P 500 lost 1.2 percent and the Dow finished flat. Asian markets had already slid, with South Korea down 3.5 percent, Japan 2.5 percent and China 2 percent, and Europe recorded its biggest fall in three weeks. Semiconductor shares led the rout: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index plunged 6.7 percent, with Lam Research down nearly 12 percent after a disappointing outlook, AMD off 8 percent, Intel down 8 percent and Nvidia 4.5 percent lower. Meta sank about 7 percent despite beating earnings expectations, as plans to raise capital spending revived fears that technology firms will load up on debt to finance AI infrastructure. Investors piled into US Treasuries, sending yields sharply lower, while Brent crude rose as much as 4 percent to above $112 a barrel — its highest since May — on Middle East tensions, and gold fell 2 percent. Reuters noted the selloff was compounded by anxiety over the global scramble to patch critical flaws in the PHP software that underpins much of the internet, after millions of hacking attempts were recorded.

France Braces for Saturday Protests: Lyon Bans All Demonstrations, Macron Calls Defence Council

Authorities in Lyon, France's third-largest city, have banned all protests on Saturday, October 24, ahead of a fresh round of nationwide cost-of-living demonstrations called by the Yellow Vest movement and trade unions. The Rhône prefecture said the decree was a direct response to the events of October 17, when rallies against high inflation were overtaken in several cities by masked youths who clashed with police and looted shops. Lyon's mayor, Grégory Doucet, said the city endured hours of "chaos" that day, with between 150 and 200 shops looted, a tourist office set on fire and around 200 arrests. Officials have also imposed three nights of curfews in parts of Paris, Lyon and other cities to protect shopping districts, and the government has promised a heavily reinforced security operation, with troops expected to support police during Saturday's demonstrations. President Emmanuel Macron announced he would chair a defence council on Friday to decide further measures, and said only union-organised protests would be allowed to proceed on Saturday. Organisers are also calling a general strike and further demonstrations on November 5.

France's Borrowing Premium Hits 15-Year High, Back at Euro-Zone Crisis Levels

The extra yield investors demand to hold French government debt instead of safe-haven German Bunds climbed to its widest level since November 2011 on Thursday, returning to levels last seen during the euro-zone sovereign debt crisis. France's benchmark 10-year yield rose 11.5 basis points to 5.11 percent, after touching 5.1415 percent — its highest since July 2002 — pushing the risk premium over Bunds to about 174 basis points. Traders blamed persistent doubts about France's ability to repair its public finances, the ongoing street protests, political uncertainty ahead of next year's presidential election and the government's difficulties in passing a budget. The selloff continued even though data showed French industrial sentiment improved in October. Analysts at Citi noted that European institutions are stronger than they were 15 years ago, which should make a collective response easier to organise, but warned that re-emerging euroscepticism constrains policymakers. ING's Charlotte de Montpellier called the confidence rebound a welcome surprise, given that the survey was conducted while the financial and political risks from the unrest were already apparent.

Latest · Middle East · Economy22 October 2026

IMF Forecasts 1.3 Percent Contraction for Middle East, North Africa and Pakistan in 2026 on War Fallout

The International Monetary Fund said on Thursday that economic output across the Middle East, North Africa and Pakistan is expected to shrink by 1.3 percent this year, blaming the impact of the US-Israeli-led war against Iran on energy production, shipping, tourism, trade and business confidence. In its regional outlook, released in Dubai, the Fund projected a sharp rebound to 6.6 percent growth in 2027, though it cautioned that output in most of the region's economies would still remain below pre-war projections. Its baseline assumes the conflict eases in early 2027 and trade gradually normalises during that year, but the Fund stressed the outlook is clouded by "exceptionally high uncertainty." The main danger, it said, is further disruption to energy production and trade routes, which could push energy prices higher; external financial conditions could also tighten if investors reassess AI-driven productivity gains and interest rates climb. The blow has landed unevenly: oil exporters directly hit by energy and shipping disruptions have suffered most, while oil importers have proved more resilient, helped by remittances, tourism, favourable financial conditions and swift policy support for consumers. Growth in the Caucasus and Central Asia is moderating toward a more sustainable pace, forecast at 5.2 percent in 2026 and 5 percent in 2027.

Latest · Americas · Trade22 October 2026

Carney Unveils C$8.7 Billion Shield for Ontario Industry, Says US Tariffs Are Hurting American Families

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney on Wednesday unveiled a C$8.7 billion (about $6.2 billion) federal-provincial plan to protect Ontario's steel, auto and critical-minerals industries from US tariffs, arguing that President Donald Trump's trade measures have raised costs for American families while tearing up a trade bargain that stood for decades. "They've raised costs for American families while putting Ontario workers and businesses at a disadvantage and under a cloud of uncertainty," Carney said, adding that while an integrated North American auto industry benefits both countries, Washington's approach has changed — "so must ours." The package includes a joint steel action fund, upgrades to auto plants for connected and electric vehicles, and new roads and faster approvals for critical-mineral projects in Ontario's north. Carney said he had spoken with Trump "for quite some time" about trade and other issues, without signalling a breakthrough, and noted Canadian auto exports to the US have fallen more than 10 percent while US exports to Canada are down over 20 percent. The announcement came as the US Embassy in Ottawa accused Canadian politicians of trying to influence American politics ahead of next month's midterm elections — a suggestion Carney and Ontario Premier Doug Ford both rejected. Trade talks between the two countries collapsed in August, after which Trump imposed 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods and Canada retaliated.

Judge Orders Trump to Sit for Deposition by December 11 in His $10 Billion BBC Lawsuit

A US federal judge on Thursday ordered President Donald Trump to sit for a deposition by December 11 in his $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the BBC, the Associated Press reported. US Magistrate Judge Enjolique Lett delivered the ruling from the bench after the British broadcaster accused Trump of stalling to avoid answering questions under oath; he cancelled a deposition scheduled for October, citing conflicts with his presidential duties. Trump sued the BBC in Florida federal court in December, alleging it misled viewers by splicing together separate parts of his January 6, 2021 speech to make it appear he had directed supporters to storm the US Capitol. The BBC has admitted the edit was misleading and apologised, but denies it is liable for defamation. In its filings, the broadcaster says Trump's side has dragged its feet during discovery, the exchange of records between the parties, and it is seeking financial records from his Mar-a-Lago club and associated business entities, arguing they are needed to contest his damages claims. Trump's lawyers counter that the requests are overbroad and irrelevant, and deny he has been avoiding a deposition, saying his duties and security needs must come first. The case is one of several Trump has filed against news organisations in what critics describe as a broader pressure campaign against coverage he dislikes.

Beatings, Cyberattacks and Threats Darken Serbia's Politics Ahead of Sunday's Election

Serbia goes to the polls on Sunday, October 25, amid what activists, student protesters and opposition politicians describe as a deepening campaign of intimidation under the country's long-time leadership. Reuters interviewed critics who recounted masked officers bundling activist and social-media commentator Marko Marjanovic into a car and holding him for two days in a police-station basement under bright lights; months earlier, he says, police beat him during anti-government protests. Former president Aleksandar Vučić, in power for 12 years, is seeking to extend his rule by running for prime minister, and his Serbian Progressive Party has repeatedly denied accusations of repression and corruption; his office and the Interior Ministry did not respond to requests for comment. The challenge he faces is the most serious of his tenure: the student-led protest movement that grew out of the November 2024 Novi Sad railway-station canopy collapse, which killed 16 people, is fielding 250 candidates of its own, and a poll this week put the students neck and neck with the ruling party. Critics say press freedom is at a new low and corruption is rising — charges that frame Sunday's vote as a pivotal test for the Balkan country.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · Economy22 October 2026

Bank of Korea Pauses at 3.00 Percent After Back-to-Back Hikes, With Another Rise Expected in November

South Korea's central bank left its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 3.00 percent on Thursday, taking a breather after quarter-point increases in both July and August — its first consecutive hikes in more than three years. The decision by the Bank of Korea's seven-member board matched the unanimous forecast of all 34 analysts surveyed by Reuters. Governor Shin Hyun Song has said a gradual pace of tightening is preferred, and reiterated at an IMF event on October 13 that policy will stay restrictive until inflation converges to target. Headline inflation eased to 2.9 percent in September from 3.1 percent in August but remains above the bank's 2 percent goal. The economy is being powered by an AI-driven export boom: shipments from January to September topped $800 billion for the first time on record. Analysts cautioned that recent market volatility, centred on the stock market, made a more hawkish signal unlikely this time. Median forecasts in the Reuters poll nevertheless point to a rise to 3.25 percent at the bank's November meeting, in line with its own projections.

US Farm Secretary Says Agency Reorganisation Blocked by a Judge Is 'Full Bore Ahead'

US Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins said on Thursday that her department is pressing ahead "full bore" with a sweeping reorganisation that a federal judge blocked last week. "We are full bore ahead in the reorganization," Rollins told reporters. "We believe in it more today than we ever have." The plan, part of the Trump administration's overhaul of the federal government, would relocate thousands of Department of Agriculture employees out of the Washington area and consolidate regional offices. On October 13, a federal judge halted the effort at several USDA agencies, ruling that the department was "reprogramming" congressional funds — spending money for a purpose other than the one it was appropriated for. The department did not immediately respond to a request for comment on how it intends to proceed in light of the ruling. The standoff sets up a fresh test of how far the administration can push its restructuring of federal agencies when courts intervene.
