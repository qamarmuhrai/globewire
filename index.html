Latest · Americas · US Midterms — First Results4 November 2026

Democrats Flip North Carolina, Make History in Georgia — and Florida Answers the Other Way

The count is moving, and the early map is splitting in both directions at once. Democrats claimed their first Senate prize when former North Carolina governor Roy Cooper beat Republican Michael Whatley — the party's first Senate win in that state since 2008, and a seat it needed on the road to retaking a chamber Republicans held 53–47. In Georgia, Keisha Lance Bottoms became the first Black woman elected governor of the state, flipping an office Democrats last held in 1998, while Senator Jon Ossoff held his seat. Republicans countered in Florida, where Representative Byron Donalds defeated Democrat David Jolly to become the state's first Black governor, and Senator Ashley Moody won her special election. Elsewhere the incumbents mostly held: Mark Warner in Virginia, John Hickenlooper in Colorado, Chris Coons in Delaware and Ed Markey in Massachusetts for the Democrats; Harriet Hageman took Wyoming's Senate seat for Republicans. Democrats also flipped House seats, including Tom Kean's in New Jersey, and need a net gain of four to take the House. The mood music favours them: exit polling put President Trump's approval at 38%, with roughly three-quarters of voters dissatisfied with the country's direction. But with all 435 House seats, more than a third of the Senate and 36 governorships in play — and western polls still closing — control of Congress remains uncalled, and may stay that way for days.

Latest · Middle East · Iran — The Chemical Claim3 November 2026

Israel Says Iran May Turn to Chemical Weapons. Washington and the Gulf Are Not Convinced.

A new argument for war is being tested in Washington, and it is landing unevenly. Over the past two weeks Israeli officials have briefed the United States and Gulf states on intelligence they say shows Iran developing ways to deliver chemical weapons — mounted on missiles, or smuggled into an enemy country — presenting the threat as more urgent than Tehran's nuclear programme, the original stated reason for the war that began on 28 February. The reception splits three ways. Some American officials took the briefings seriously; others read them as a pressure campaign by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who has been urging President Trump to resume large-scale strikes, potentially within days of the midterms — US Central Command is reported to have a plan ready as early as Wednesday. Gulf officials who saw the material, which consisted of Israeli assessments rather than physical evidence, called it unserious. Inside the US intelligence community there is little dispute that Iran is developing chemical weapons; the argument is over whether its leaders would actually use them if not attacked again. Tehran denies holding such weapons and calls the accusations psychological warfare. One detail undercuts the alarm at home: Israel's own Home Front Command drills this week have not centred on a chemical threat. With Qatar still mediating and Iran's Hormuz conditions unmet, the claim now sits where the next round of the war may be decided — in the gap between intelligence and persuasion.

Latest · Europe · Ukraine — Power Grid Under Fire3 November 2026

289 Drones, 76 Missiles, Three Power Plants Down: Russia's Message Before Abu Dhabi

A week before peace talks resume, Russia delivered its brief by air. Overnight it launched 289 drones alongside 17 ballistic and 59 cruise missiles at Ukraine's energy system, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said, striking from Odesa in the south to Rivne in the west and cutting power across Kyiv and at least six other regions; DTEK, the country's largest private generator, said three of its five thermal plants were knocked offline, with water and transport in the capital disrupted. Ukraine said a major refinery in Russia's Yaroslavl was set ablaze in return. The barrage landed less than two days after the war's longest local truce — the six-day pause around occupied Oleshky, brokered with the help of US First Lady Melania Trump to evacuate civilians — expired, and a day after Russian bombs hit a school in Komyshuvakha serving as an aid point, killing at least 33 people, with a second strike on the shelter where survivors had gathered. Zelenskyy, standing beside Chancellor Friedrich Merz in Berlin, said the pattern showed Moscow intent on killing rather than building on the truce; Russia says it struck military units in the area. The European Commission hosts the US, Ukraine, NATO and European allies in Brussels on Wednesday to work on security guarantees before the US-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi on 10–11 November — a diplomatic sequence now running against a grid going dark.

Latest · Europe · Aviation — Hoax Threats4 November 2026

Seven Flights, One Cruise Ship, No Bombs: A Day of Hoaxes Across Europe's Skies

Europe's airports spent Tuesday treating threats as real until proven otherwise — and every one proved false. At least seven passenger flights were searched after bomb warnings: a LOT Polish Airlines flight from London Heathrow to Warsaw, carrying 181 people, was diverted to the capital's smaller Modlin airport, which closed while emergency services worked; an SAS flight from Copenhagen bound for Warsaw was also targeted; Ryanair services from Manchester to Murcia, Lisbon to Rome and Dublin to Rome were inspected on landing, the Dublin flight escorted in by Italian Air Force jets; and a Wizz Air flight from Bergamo was checked in Sofia. London's Metropolitan Police said it had been alerted to threats against four aircraft linked to Heathrow and another at Manchester, finding no security issues. A Panama-flagged cruise ship was also evacuated on the Greek island of Syros. The wave followed a Monday incident in which a Wizz Air flight from Luton to Sofia diverted to Budapest under fighter escort. No authority has named who is behind the threats or whether they are coordinated, but they arrive as European officials blame Russia and Iran for a broader campaign of hybrid disruption — a context that turns even a hoax into a costly, continent-wide rehearsal.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · North Korea — Another Launch4 November 2026

Projectile Over the Sea of Japan: Pyongyang's Test Cadence Quickens Again

North Korea fired at least one unidentified projectile off its east coast into the Sea of Japan on Wednesday, South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff said, the latest in a dense run of weapons trials. Seoul's military is still analysing the type and range; Tokyo issued no public alert, as it usually does for launches that could threaten Japan, suggesting a shorter-range system. The launch follows days of announcements from Pyongyang: strategic cruise missiles fitted with cluster warheads on Monday, a tactical ballistic missile with a similar warhead on Saturday, and more than ten rounds from a 600mm multiple rocket launcher — classed by Seoul, Washington and Tokyo as short-range ballistic missiles — the previous Thursday. State media has framed the cluster-warhead work, overseen by General Kim Jong Sik, as routine research, with claims that one warhead can devastate 12 to 13 hectares. Underneath the cadence is friction: a joint Seoul–UN investigation concluded North Korea planted the landmines that exploded in the Demilitarised Zone, a finding Pyongyang angrily denies, and the tests come weeks before a possible encounter between Kim Jong Un and President Trump at the APEC summit in Shenzhen. Each launch adds data; together they add leverage.

Latest · Global Affairs · World Court — Rohingya Ruling Set3 November 2026

19 November at The Hague: The World Court Rules on Whether Myanmar Committed Genocide

The International Court of Justice will deliver its judgment on 19 November in the case accusing Myanmar of genocide against the Rohingya, the court announced on Tuesday, with judges beginning to read the ruling at 08:00 GMT. The case was brought by Gambia in 2019, two years after a military campaign drove hundreds of thousands of Rohingya Muslims from Rakhine State. It carries weight far beyond one country: this will be only the third genocide judgment in the court's history, and the first in more than a decade. Legal observers expect its reasoning on evidence, intent and state responsibility to shape the other genocide case now before the court — South Africa's 2023 claim against Israel over Gaza, which Israel denies. ICJ judgments are binding on states but the court has no police force of its own; enforcement runs through politics and the UN Security Council. For the roughly one million Rohingya still living in refugee camps in Bangladesh, the ruling will not by itself open a road home — but it will settle, in law, the word that the world has argued over for nine years.

Latest · Africa · Trade — AGOA Suspension4 November 2026

Washington Pulls the Duty-Free Ladder: South Africa and Rwanda Lose AGOA From January

The United States will suspend South Africa and Rwanda from the African Growth and Opportunity Act from 1 January 2027, US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has told Congress — ending, for two of the programme's most visible beneficiaries, the duty-free access to the American market that has anchored US economic engagement with Africa since 2000. The exposure is uneven but real: South Africa was the scheme's largest beneficiary, shipping about $1.7 billion of goods under AGOA in 2025, with auto parts and wine among the sectors now bracing for tariffs; Rwanda's coffee exports are also in the line. Pretoria's trade ministry said late on Monday it would support affected sectors while continuing to pursue trade and investment ties with Washington. The timing compounds the pressure at home: South Africans vote in local elections on Wednesday with municipal services in collapse in many towns, and business groups have warned of job losses as preferences lapse. AGOA itself was built as leverage — eligibility tied to governance and market conditions — and Washington is now using it as such, on the eve of an APEC season in which African exporters are being courted just as actively by China.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · Vietnam — Floods3 November 2026

37 Dead, 64,000 Livestock Lost — and Another Typhoon on the Way Into Central Vietnam

Vietnam's flood disaster is still rising on two ledgers at once: the human one and the forecast. The national disaster authority counted 37 people dead, five missing and 78 injured as of Tuesday morning after days of heavy rain and flooding across the central region, with nearly 7,900 hectares of paddy and crops submerged or damaged and more than 64,000 livestock and poultry killed or swept away. The government has ordered ministries and provinces to act on the prime minister's 2 November dispatch and move faster on relief and repairs in the inundated provinces. What makes the next 48 hours dangerous is what sits offshore: a new typhoon is threatening the same saturated ground, where rivers are already high, hillsides loosened and drainage overwhelmed. Central Vietnam floods most years; the compounding is the story — a second storm arriving before the first one's water has left, turning recovery work into preparation for the next impact.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · Afghanistan — Earthquake3 November 2026

Northern Afghanistan Counts 24 Dead and More Than 630 Injured After the Night Quake

A magnitude 6.3 earthquake in northern Afghanistan has killed at least 24 people and injured more than 630, the country's National Disaster Management Authority said on Monday, with most injuries described as minor but rescue teams still reaching remote districts where communications are patchy. Early provincial counts from Samangan and Balkh — including deaths in Aybak and in Balkh's Khulm district — pointed to the pattern Afghan quakes so often follow: mud-brick and timber homes collapsing at night in mountainous terrain, and a toll that climbs as assessors arrive. Afghanistan sits on active fault lines in the Hindu Kush and has almost no margin for a disaster of this kind — its health system is aid-dependent, funding has fallen sharply since 2021, and winter is approaching in the north. Officials warned the casualty figures may rise as damage assessments come in from cut-off areas; for now, the emergency is a race between rescue teams, distance and the cold.

Latest · Africa · Sport — Dakar 20263 November 2026

Africa's First Olympic Event Is Running on Borrowed Cloud — Senegal Keeps It Afterwards

The Dakar 2026 Youth Olympic Games, under way since 31 October and running to 13 November, are the first Olympic event ever staged on African soil — 2,700 athletes aged 17 and under, 35 sports, venues across Dakar, Diamniadio and Saly. The infrastructure story runs underneath the medals: Alibaba Cloud is providing the Games' private cloud platform, hosting ticketing, e-visa processing and venue access control, with its Qwen AI models supporting more than 200 languages including Wolof and Kongo. When the flame goes out, the platform stays — it will be handed to Senegal as a national cloud for local businesses and developers, and the company's tech academy is offering free cloud training to Senegalese residents under 35. For the IOC the arrangement is a template for taking the Games to hosts without legacy data centres; for Senegal it is a bet that the most durable thing a two-week youth tournament can leave behind is not a stadium, but a server stack and the people trained to run it.
