Latest · Middle East · Hormuz Diplomacy1 November 2026

Iran Says a Two-Day Plan to Reopen Hormuz Is Under Discussion — Even as Both Sides Trade Fresh Strike Threats

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, has told Al Jazeera that talks with Qatari mediators have produced a shortened version of Tehran's earlier seven-day proposal for reopening the Strait of Hormuz: if Washington carries out an agreed set of steps on the first day, shipping through the strait would resume on the second. He said Tehran has studied a letter from US Vice President JD Vance in high-level meetings and set out its conditions, and a senior Iranian security figure described the tone of that letter as completely changed and a positive sign. The diplomacy is unfolding against sharp warnings. Iran's supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has ordered his commanders to answer any new threat immediately, effectively and without geographical limits, and to update a target list covering American and Israeli assets. In Florida, President Trump said the United States could hit what remains of Iran's nuclear programme, including the Pickaxe Mountain site, in ten minutes, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio stressed that no decision on fresh strikes has been taken and that a diplomatic outcome remains possible. Shipping through Hormuz, which normally carries about a fifth of global oil consumption, has been heavily disrupted, with a record number of reported incidents and a US naval blockade redirecting commercial vessels.

Latest · Europe · Germany Protests1 November 2026

Germany's Youth-Led Protests Turn Violent as Rioters Loot Shops in Frankfurt and Police Use Water Cannon

Demonstrations organised by the youth movement calling itself “Gen Z regelt!” drew tens of thousands across Germany on Saturday before turning violent in the evening, in a wave of unrest inspired by similar protests in France and Italy. In Frankfurt, masked rioters looted shops and fires burned in the streets, while police in several cities used water cannon and pepper spray against groups throwing fireworks, stones, bottles and, in some places, Molotov cocktails. Several officers were injured, according to authorities. Organisers put nationwide turnout at about 120,000. The grievances echo those driving the wider European movement: the high cost of living, poorly maintained schools, spending cuts and a sense that politicians decide without regard for younger people. The violence in Germany came on the same day France staged its third consecutive weekend of mass protests over living costs under a deployment of some 70,000 security personnel, underlining how the youth-led anger is now spreading across the continent rather than staying in one country.

Latest · Europe · Ukraine War1 November 2026

Ukraine Says Russia Used the Oleshky Truce to Rotate Troops, as 134 Drones Rain Down and a $2.1 Billion Patriot Sale Is Approved

A day after evacuees from Russian-occupied Oleshky reached Ukrainian-held territory under a rare humanitarian ceasefire, Ukraine's southern forces accused Russia of exploiting the pause — rotating units, fortifying positions, laying anti-drone protection on roads and bringing in ammunition — and of dropping munitions from drones in an effort to make Kyiv look responsible for violations. The truce, credited to mediation involving First Lady Melania Trump and Pope Leo XIV, is due to expire on Sunday evening. Elsewhere the war did not pause: Ukraine's air force said Russia attacked overnight with 134 drones, including jet-powered Shaheds, of which 105 were shot down or jammed, with hits recorded at 20 locations and debris at 10 more; a drone strike on a home in Kharkiv injured five people. Separate reporting put hundreds of thousands without power after strikes on energy facilities. Against that backdrop, Washington has approved the possible sale of 500 advanced Patriot PAC-3 interceptors to Ukraine for an estimated $2.1 billion, to be paid for by Canada, Germany and Norway through the JUMPSTART programme together with EU-financed funds, with Lockheed Martin as principal contractor. President Zelenskyy welcomed the decision but warned the missiles, yet to be manufactured, may not arrive for years, and urged allies to release existing stockpiles for this winter instead.

Latest · Business · Binance Heist1 November 2026

FBI Formally Blames North Korea for the $6.3 Billion Binance Theft as the Exchange Says It Has Covered 90% of Losses

The FBI has officially attributed the theft of more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about 29 October to North Korea, naming the TraderTraitor group — the same unit blamed for the $1.5 billion Bybit theft in February 2025 and linked to the Lazarus Group. In a joint statement with the Secret Service and the IRS criminal division, the bureau said the attackers have been converting the stolen funds mainly into Ethereum and are expected to launder them further through decentralised finance and instant-swap services. It published hundreds of suspect addresses — more than 600 on Ethereum alone — and urged exchanges, node operators and analytics firms to block transactions derived from them. Binance, for its part, says it has now replenished about 90% of what was taken, buying replacement tokens with money from its $1 billion Secure Asset Fund for Users, a futures insurance fund and its corporate treasury, using half of the SAFU fund for the purpose according to co-founder Yi He. The exchange has set aside more than $6.34 billion in company funds in total, says users will bear no losses, and aims to restore withdrawals around Sunday. Its co-chief executive has described the breach as a complex attack involving a compromised laptop and evidence-deleting malware. President Trump, asked about the hack on Saturday, said his administration would talk to North Korea about it.

Latest · Americas · Trump v. Wall Street Journal1 November 2026

Judge Throws Out Trump's $10 Billion Lawsuit Against The Wall Street Journal for Good, Calling It 'Grasping at Straws'

A federal judge in Miami has dismissed President Trump's multibillion-dollar defamation lawsuit against The Wall Street Journal and Rupert Murdoch for a second time — and this time permanently. US District Judge Darrin Gayles ruled on Saturday that Trump had again failed to plausibly allege that the newspaper acted with actual malice, the standard a public figure must meet under the Supreme Court's 1964 New York Times v. Sullivan decision, and dismissed the case with prejudice, meaning it cannot be refiled. Gayles described the president's arguments as conclusory, vague and at times inconsistent with the article itself, and rejected the claim that the Journal should have withheld its story after Trump denied it, saying such a rule would let any public figure block unfavourable reporting simply by issuing a denial. The case stemmed from a July 2025 story about a sexually suggestive letter bearing Trump's name in a 2003 birthday album compiled for Jeffrey Epstein. Trump denied writing the letter and sued Dow Jones, News Corp, its executives and two reporters, seeking at least $10 billion. He first had the suit dismissed in April but was allowed to amend it; the amended version, filed in May, has now met the same fate, leaving an appeal as his remaining option.

Trump Heads to Kansas and Iowa in a Reinstated Final Push as Polls Show Him Underwater in 42 States

With voting on Tuesday set to decide control of Congress, President Trump has reversed an earlier cancellation and will rally in Kansas and Iowa on Sunday, telling supporters in Florida that local Republicans persuaded him an in-person visit would beat a tele-rally — while conceding that 28 straight days of campaigning is a lot and that his voice is operating at about half strength. The closing days are also being fought in court, with the Justice Department's challenges over voter rolls and California mail-in ballots — more than 40% of the names it flagged as possible noncitizen voters there do not appear on the state's rolls, according to a filing by an immigrant-rights group — and California's governor urging federal judges to scrutinise any attempt to seize election materials before results are certified.

Latest · Europe · Britain and Slavery31 October 2026

Burnham Opens the Door to a Formal British Apology for Slavery — but Rules Out Reparations Ahead of the Commonwealth Summit

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is open to calls for Britain to formally apologise for its role in the transatlantic slave trade, while firmly rejecting financial reparations, in a Guardian article published ahead of the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua and Barbuda. Britain, he wrote, needs to face up to the past to safeguard the Commonwealth, and he offered a structured dialogue with those affected, in which listening to calls for a state apology would be an important part. He expressed sorrow and regret for the suffering of millions — at least 12.5 million Africans were abducted and sold, with Britain transporting an estimated 3.2 million — but argued that people in Britain today cannot be held liable for wrongs committed in a different age under different laws. Britain has never formally apologised for slavery, which it outlawed across its empire in 1833. Instead of reparations, Burnham proposed an international debt-restructuring drive for the poorest Commonwealth countries, to be led by former prime minister Gordon Brown, noting that many vulnerable states spend more on debt interest than on healthcare. Caribbean leaders welcomed the openness to an apology while insisting their position on reparatory justice is unchanged; King Charles, touring the Caribbean before the summit, will not issue a formal apology on his trip, Buckingham Palace has said.

London Police Arrest 139 Outside the US Embassy for Backing Banned Group Palestine Action

Police arrested 139 people in London on Saturday after hundreds gathered outside the United States embassy in defiance of the ban on Palestine Action, the direct-action group proscribed as a terrorist organisation in July 2025. The Metropolitan Police said most arrests were for expressing support for a proscribed organisation — an offence carrying up to 14 years in prison — after officers began detaining people at the lunchtime gathering in Nine Elms, in some cases carrying away protesters who refused to walk. Many of those taking part were retirees holding placards reading “I oppose genocide. I support Palestine Action.” A parallel vigil outside the US consulate in Edinburgh passed without arrests. The protest was timed ahead of a Supreme Court appeal on 4 November by the group's co-founder against the proscription, which a High Court judge earlier ruled unlawful before the Court of Appeal upheld the ban in June. It was also the first mass protest against the ban since Prime Minister Andy Burnham took office in July.

Latest · Middle East · Yemen31 October 2026

UN Demands an Independent Investigation After a Yemeni Staff Member Dies in Houthi Custody

The United Nations' human rights chief, Volker Türk, has demanded an urgent, independent and thorough investigation into the death of Mohammad el Shami, a Yemeni staff member of the UN Human Rights Office who had been held by the Houthis since 6 June 2024. Türk called the death utterly devastating and said el Shami's family were informed only recently, more than a month after he died, and have received no explanation of the cause or circumstances. He called on the Houthi authorities to release all medical and detention records, preserve evidence and allow independent scrutiny, insisting there must be accountability for wrongdoing. El Shami is the second UN employee known to have died in Houthi custody, after a World Food Programme worker detained in January 2025 died the following month. The UN says 71 of its personnel remain in Houthi detention, including seven from Türk's own office, alongside humanitarian and civil society workers; the Houthis accuse those held of spying for the United States and Israel, charges the UN rejects. Yemen's internationally recognised government held the Houthis fully responsible and joined the call for the immediate, unconditional release of all remaining detainees. There was no immediate comment from the Houthis.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · China Economy31 October 2026

China's Factory Activity Contracts for the Third Time in Four Months as Orders and Exports Weaken

China's official manufacturing purchasing managers' index fell to 49.3 in October from 50.1 in September, below the 50 mark that separates expansion from contraction and short of a forecast of 50.0, official data released on Saturday showed. It was the sector's third contraction in four months, driven by soft demand at home and cooling exports after Christmas-related overseas orders that had been front-loaded into September faded; a week-long national holiday at the start of the month also weighed on activity. Sub-indexes for overall new orders and new export orders both stayed in contraction, and production and purchasing slipped from expansion into contraction. The non-manufacturing index, covering services and construction, also fell, to 49.7 from 50.2. The weakness was uneven: large factories still expanded slightly at 50.2, while medium and small manufacturers fell to 48.4 and 47.5, and high-technology manufacturing remained a bright spot at 51.3, helped by the artificial intelligence boom. Analysts called weak demand the central problem and urged faster public investment and support. Beijing is targeting growth of 4.5% to 5% this year, its lowest goal in decades, even as the country's trade surplus remains on track to top last year's $1.2 trillion.

Latest · Africa · Russia–Africa Summit1 November 2026

48 African Nations Gather in Moscow as Russia Tries to Turn Trade Ties Into Long-Term Investment

The third Russia–Africa Summit has concluded in Moscow, drawing delegations from 48 African countries, 22 of them led by heads of state or government, and co-chaired by President Vladimir Putin and African Union chairperson Évariste Ndayishimiye of Burundi. Held on 28 and 29 October in Moscow rather than in Africa as once promised, the meeting ranged across politics, investment, technology, humanitarian issues and culture. Its central economic question, analysts noted, is whether Russia can convert growing trade flows with the continent into durable investment in sectors where African states still face deep capacity gaps — logistics, energy, raw-material processing and digital infrastructure. Any Russian offers will compete with Gulf sovereign wealth funds, Asian development banks, European agencies and the African Development Bank. European commentators were sceptical, arguing the summit's televised pageantry masks limited economic resources as Russia remains absorbed by its war in Ukraine, while African leaders attending were seen as pursuing pragmatic goals — diplomatic leverage and financial preferences — of their own.
