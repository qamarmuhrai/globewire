Latest · Europe · Spain Train Crash Inquiry28 October 2026

Spain Crash Inquiry: Controller Questioned After a Train Was Cleared to Run at Full Speed Despite a Failed Safety System — Sánchez Vows to Find Any Negligence

Spain's investigation into Wednesday's high-speed train collision near Urda in Toledo province, which killed at least 68 people and injured more than 100, is focusing on how a Renfe service was allowed to resume at normal speed after the LZB train-control system failed at about 8:30 a.m. and halted traffic along a 63-kilometre stretch. Transport Minister Óscar Puente said network operator Adif authorised the train to continue at 8:36 a.m., but that after such a failure it should have been limited to 40 km/h — slow enough to stop short of any obstruction — and instead it travelled about 25 kilometres at high speed before striking the stationary Iryo train at 8:53 a.m. The Renfe train was carrying 397 people and the Iryo 147; 44 of the dead were on the Iryo, whose rear carriage was destroyed, and 77 people were taken to hospital, 15 of them seriously injured. Among the dead was Fátima Báñez, 59, a former minister of labour and social economy. The Guardia Civil has questioned the controller who gave the clearance, and Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, speaking at the scene, promised a thorough investigation and decisive action if negligence or safety failures are found. It was Spain's third deadly rail incident this year and its deadliest crash since 2013, coming nine months after a collision on the same Madrid–Andalusia line killed 46 people.

Latest · Americas · US Federal Reserve28 October 2026

Federal Reserve Holds Rates at 3.75%–4% in a 10–2 Vote Less Than a Week Before the Midterms — Two Officials Wanted Another Hike

The US Federal Reserve kept its benchmark interest rate unchanged on Wednesday in a range of 3.75% to 4.00%, pausing after last month's quarter-point increase — its first rise in three years — to assess the effect of that move and the path of inflation before next week's midterm elections. The decision passed 10–2, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissenting in favour of another quarter-point rise. The Federal Open Market Committee said inflation "remains elevated" — consumer prices rose 3.7% year on year in September, the fastest pace in five months, driven largely by energy costs linked to the Iran war — while describing economic activity as expanding at a strong pace, with job gains keeping up with workforce growth. Chair Kevin Warsh, who took over the central bank in May, offered no guidance on December, saying he does not believe in prejudging meetings and that the committee had not yet achieved price stability but was "working on it." Traders, who had already expected a further increase at the Fed's final meeting of the year, grew more confident after the decision that one will come in December.

Latest · Global Affairs · World Bank Outlook28 October 2026

World Bank: Global Commodity Prices Will Surge 23% in 2026 as Oil Supply Suffers Its Biggest Drop in Four Decades Outside the 2020 Recession

Global commodity prices are on course to jump 23% in 2026, the World Bank said on Wednesday in its latest Commodity Markets Outlook, blaming higher energy prices and disrupted cargo shipping during the Middle East war, problems with grain shipments in the Black Sea and strong demand for safe-haven assets such as gold. Oil, natural gas, food and metals have all risen sharply, and the bank expects global oil supply to fall 5.2% this year — the largest decline in four decades outside the 2020 global recession — after wartime depletion of global oil stocks. Prices are forecast to ease by 7% in 2027, but the bank warned the outlook could worsen if the Middle East conflict escalates or damage to Russian oil infrastructure continues, and that forecasts for the worst El Niño weather pattern since 1950 could keep food prices high, raising the risk of further inflation and tighter monetary policy. Deputy chief economist Ayhan Kose said commodity markets were being hit by several major shocks at once, threatening inflation, food security and growth, with the poorest countries — already carrying heavy debts — most exposed. The report lands two days before G20 foreign ministers gather in Atlanta, Georgia, for talks focused on the wars in the Middle East and Ukraine.

Latest · Middle East · Cost of the Iran War28 October 2026

Pentagon Puts the US Bill for the Iran War at $47.1 Billion — Up More Than 25% Since July, With $29 Billion Needed Just to Replace Spent Munitions

The cost of US military operations against Iran reached at least $47.1 billion by mid-October, according to Pentagon figures provided to lawmakers and obtained by Reuters and CBS News — more than 25% above the $37.5 billion estimate Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth gave the Senate in July, and up from $43.6 billion in early September. Central Command's breakdown puts about $29 billion down to replenishing munitions expended since the war began in February, $6.1 billion to replacing lost equipment including aircraft and many drones, and $11.9 billion to additional operational costs such as supporting deployed personnel; the total does not include repairing US bases in the Middle East damaged by Iranian missile and drone attacks. The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office separately estimated about $38 billion in costs through 1 August and projected a further $2–3 billion a month depending on the intensity of fighting. Washington initially relied on expensive precision weapons to suppress Iranian defences, but an April ceasefire gave Tehran time to recover buried weapons and restore air defences, and the US has since imposed a naval blockade while officials plan for a possible resumption of heavy fighting. The Pentagon had not publicly released the figures and did not respond to requests for comment; the rising cost and its effect on energy prices are weighing on Republicans six days before the 3 November midterms.

Latest · Middle East · Israel After the Election28 October 2026

Deadlock Leaves Netanyahu in Office as Caretaker — With Legal Power to Take War Decisions, Experts Say, but a Duty of Restraint

Benjamin Netanyahu is set to remain Israel's prime minister in a caretaker capacity after Tuesday's election left neither his right-wing bloc nor the rival camp led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot able to reach the 61 seats needed to govern the 120-seat Knesset. Netanyahu has called for a strong right-wing national government, while Eisenkot says voters rejected the 77-year-old leader and has urged him to retire; neither man has claimed victory, and with hundreds of thousands of double-envelope ballots still being counted, coalition talks are expected to drag on, with another election possible if no majority emerges. Legal experts say a caretaker government in Israel formally holds the same authority as a regular one — including, in principle, decisions to go to war, even against Iran — because courts have historically avoided intervening in such decisions and parliamentary oversight is weakest in the weeks after an election before Knesset committees form. Analysts add, however, that caretaker governments are bound by a duty of restraint and face tighter limits in areas such as senior appointments, acting broadly only where there is a clear public interest. Eisenkot has acknowledged Netanyahu remains in office for now but warned against security decisions being used for political ends, an accusation the prime minister's office has previously rejected.

Latest · Europe · Ukraine Diplomacy28 October 2026

Finland and Norway Break Europe's Silence With a 90-Minute Call to Putin — "Ending the War Requires Direct Discussions"

Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre held a 90-minute telephone call with Russian President Vladimir Putin on Tuesday, urging an end to the war in Ukraine in a rare direct approach by Western European leaders, who have largely shunned contact with the Kremlin. The two leaders described the conversation as frank and substantive, said the outcome of the war directly affects European security, and stressed that ending it requires direct discussions; Støre acknowledged big differences in views. Stubb said the idea came from an Oslo meeting on 20 October with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and US envoy Steve Witkoff — with Jared Kushner joining remotely — and that both Washington and Kyiv supported opening the channel; Witkoff afterwards travelled to St Petersburg to meet Putin, and the Nordic leaders briefed Zelenskyy on their call. The Kremlin's account showed no softening: Putin blamed what he called the EU's and NATO's confrontational policy for prolonging the fighting, dwelt on the war's stated root causes, and the sides agreed only to keep in telephone contact. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen voiced scepticism and suggested the call had not been coordinated with other European governments, while Ukraine's foreign minister Andrii Sybiha said it had been coordinated with Kyiv. The outreach comes ahead of planned Russian, Ukrainian and US talks in Abu Dhabi next month, the first since February.

Trump Says a Putin–Zelenskyy Meeting Will Be Arranged "Very Soon" — but the Kremlin Insists Putin Will Meet Only to Sign a Ready Peace Deal

President Donald Trump has said direct talks between Vladimir Putin and Volodymyr Zelenskyy will be set up "very soon" to end the war in Ukraine, and that he is also meeting China's President Xi Jinping on nuclear disarmament, an effort he suggested Russia could join. The Kremlin, however, restated its long-standing condition after Putin's calls with the Norwegian and Finnish leaders: aide Yuri Ushakov said Putin is prepared to meet Zelenskyy only once a peace agreement is ready to be signed, and that the Ukrainian president should come to Moscow if he wants direct discussions. Zelenskyy challenged Putin to direct talks in June, proposing Switzerland, Türkiye or Arab countries as venues; Putin dismissed the appeal and told his military to keep working. Trump, who promised as a candidate in 2024 to settle the war within 24 hours, has grown frustrated with Kyiv in recent weeks, blaming Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure for higher US fuel prices, though the Iran war is widely seen as the bigger driver. Battlefield lines remain largely static, negotiations since Trump returned to office have produced no settlement, and rising fuel, healthcare and housing costs have put his Republican Party on the defensive ahead of next week's elections.

India Invites a Pakistani Minister to a Delhi Forum for the First Time Since Operation Sindoor — an SCO Cultural Dialogue in November

India has invited Pakistan's National Heritage and Culture Minister Aurangzeb Khan Khichi to the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation's inaugural Civilisational Dialogue Forum in New Delhi on 20–21 November, the first invitation to a senior Pakistani minister since the military hostilities of Operation Sindoor in May 2025, which followed the Pahalgam terror attack. If Khichi attends, it would be the first visit to India by a senior Pakistani minister since then foreign minister Bilawal Bhutto Zardari travelled to Goa for an SCO foreign ministers' meeting in May 2023. The invitation is part of India's obligation as host to invite all SCO members — the forum, proposed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the Tianjin summit in September 2025 and formally announced by Defence Minister Rajnath Singh at the SCO heads-of-government meeting in Dushanbe on 20 October, aims to strengthen people-to-people and cultural ties across the bloc's ten members. Bilateral ties have otherwise been frozen for a decade, and at Dushanbe Singh paired the cultural outreach with a call for a firm, consistent approach to terrorism with no double standards — remarks made with Pakistan's Deputy Prime Minister Ishaq Dar in attendance. Pakistan assumed the rotating chairmanship of the SCO Council of Heads of State for 2026–27 on 1 September and is due to host the leaders' summit next year; Islamabad has yet to confirm whether Khichi will travel.

Latest · Americas · Obituary28 October 2026

Dolores Huerta, Who Co-Founded the United Farm Workers and Gave It the Slogan "Sí, Se Puede," Dies at 96

Dolores Huerta, the labour organiser who co-founded the United Farm Workers with Cesar Chavez and spent six decades fighting for farmworkers, women and immigrants, died on Wednesday at her home in Bakersfield, California, at the age of 96; a family spokesman said the cause was cancer. A former schoolteacher who turned to organising after seeing her pupils arrive hungry, Huerta co-founded the National Farm Workers Association with Chavez in 1962 and became the union's chief negotiator, strike organiser and lobbyist, helping win contracts that brought farmworkers job security, healthcare, rest periods, field toilets, clean drinking water and protection from pesticides, and campaigning for the landmark 1975 California Agricultural Labor Relations Act. In 1972 in Arizona she answered a supporter's "no se puede" — it can't be done — with "Sí, se puede," the phrase that became the union's rallying cry and, decades later, Barack Obama's 2008 campaign slogan; Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2012. She remained active into her 90s, registering voters and campaigning against the Trump administration's immigration policies, and this year she was among several women who publicly accused Chavez — who died in 1993 — of sexual abuse, saying he had pressured her into sex in 1960 and raped her in 1966, accounts she said she had kept secret for fear no one would believe her. Her family asked for privacy while announcing that memorial plans will follow, and the UFW said the greatest tribute to her life is for her organising work to continue.

Latest · Americas · Canada29 October 2026

Alberta's Official Referendum Count Is Due Thursday After About 76% of Voters Chose to Remain Part of Canada

Elections Alberta is scheduled to publish the official results on Thursday of the province's first-ever separation referendum, after preliminary returns from the non-binding vote on 19 October showed about 76% of Albertans choosing to remain part of Canada against roughly 24% who wanted the provincial government to begin the constitutional process toward a later, binding vote on independence. More than 1.4 million ballots were cast, and the count — conducted by hand, beginning with the separation question — produced a margin wide enough that broadcasters projected the outcome on election night. The question asked whether Alberta should remain a province of Canada or whether its government should commence the legal process required to hold a binding referendum on separation, so even a separatist win would not itself have taken the oil-rich province out of the federation. Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton and campaigned for unity in Calgary, called the result a clear choice to build together, while Premier Danielle Smith, who called the vote to settle the question, said she was pleased residents had rejected secession. The referendum grew out of long-running western grievances over federal energy and climate policy, pipelines and equalisation payments; leaders of the separatist Alberta Prosperity Project say their campaign will continue despite the defeat.
