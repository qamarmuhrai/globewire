Latest · Americas · Paraguay — Senate Votes Removal7 November 2026

Removed: Paraguay’s Senate Votes Peña Out over the Ueno Bank Collapse

Paraguay’s crisis reached its verdict late on Friday. Reuters reported that the Senate voted to remove President Santiago Peña from office over his connection to the collapse of digital lender Ueno Bank, in rapid impeachment proceedings held despite the resignation he tendered a day earlier. The day had begun with senators voting 39 to 3, with two abstentions, to refuse Peña a 72-hour delay to prepare his defence; when he failed to appear, the chamber voted to press on with the juicio político without him. The sequence matters legally as much as politically: a resignation accepted by Congress would have made the 47-year-old a senator for life, with the protections that status carries, while conviction by impeachment closes that path. Peña had been impeached unanimously, 78 to 0, by the lower house on Thursday minutes after announcing he would step down, and a joint session called to accept the resignation collapsed when Colorado Party lawmakers walked out and broke the quorum. Around the politics, the financial wreckage continues: the attorney general’s three criminal investigations are open, six Ueno executives including founder Miguel Vázquez are in preventive detention, 27 people face charges, and capped deposit-guarantee payments have begun for customers frozen out of their accounts. Vice President Pedro Alliana stands next in the line of succession.

Worst Week Since June: Chips Bounce, the Dow Slips, and the Week Still Belongs to the Rout

Wall Street’s Friday close settled the argument about the week. The Nasdaq Composite rose 1.09 per cent to 24,579.42 and the S&P 500 added 0.31 per cent to 7,376.86 as chip shares recovered part of Thursday’s collapse — AMD jumped about 11 per cent, the semiconductor fund SMH gained 3.6 per cent and Nvidia rose 2.78 per cent — but the Dow Jones fell 0.46 per cent to 51,285.73, held back after a red-hot jobs report made a December Federal Reserve rate hike the market’s base case. The weekly ledger is stark: the S&P 500 lost 2.7 per cent, the Nasdaq 3.4 per cent and the Dow 0.9 per cent from their 30 October closes, the worst week since June, with the technology sector down about 8 per cent in its fifth straight weekly loss. The day’s fuel was the October payrolls report — 165,000 jobs against roughly 65,000 expected, unemployment down to 4.1 per cent — which lifted the implied odds of a December hike to about 78 per cent and left the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.859 per cent. Fear cooled without vanishing: the VIX fell about 9 per cent to 25.58, gold eased to near $4,074 and WTI crude settled close to $90.43. Axon was the day’s casualty, plunging about 18 per cent after missing estimates and cutting guidance.

Latest · Americas · Vatican — 30,000 in Independence Square7 November 2026

“I Feel Fairly Good”: Leo Lands in Montevideo and Turns His Own Surgery into a Plea for Health Care for All

Pope Leo XIV is on Uruguayan soil. His chartered ITA Airways flight touched down at Montevideo’s Carrasco airport a little before 4:20 p.m. on Friday after a 13-hour, 6,900-mile crossing from Rome, and the 71-year-old pontiff — barely three weeks past lung-cancer surgery — told reporters travelling with him that he feels “fairly good” and was convinced the journey had to go forward. President Yamandú Orsi and two children with flowers met him on the tarmac. At Plaza Independencia, before a crowd the Vatican estimated at no fewer than 30,000, Leo urged South American nations to tackle the region’s problems together and set an example of cooperation, then drew on his own illness and on Uruguay’s national hero José Gervasio Artigas to argue for universal access to health care and protection of the most vulnerable. The evening closed with Vespers at the Metropolitan Cathedral. It is the first papal visit to Uruguay since John Paul II in 1988, in Latin America’s most secular country, and only the opening of a demanding 12-day journey: some 31,000 kilometres, ten cities and 36 speeches across Uruguay, Argentina and Peru, starting Saturday with Mass in Paysandú and a youth vigil expected to draw 60,000 to the Estadio Centenario.

Latest · Africa · South Africa — 95% Counted, 110 Hung Councils7 November 2026

110 Councils Hung: at 95% Counted, South Africa Faces a Coalition Maze with No Way Around It

By Friday night South Africa’s count had reached about 95 per cent of voting districts, and the headline was no longer only the ANC’s fall but the shape of what replaces it: 110 of the country’s municipal councils are hung, with no party holding an outright majority. The Electoral Commission dashboard put the ANC on 31.22 per cent with 8,464,323 votes, outright control of just 44 councils and 2,753 seats — its weakest showing since 1994, down from 45.6 per cent in 2021. The Democratic Alliance held second place on 24.43 per cent with 6,615,253 votes, 1,204 seats and 11 councils controlled outright, leading in six of the eight metros. Jacob Zuma’s MK Party was third on 9.61 per cent with 2,602,676 votes and 614 seats, and first in KwaZulu-Natal and eThekwini. Turnout, notably, rose to about 51.3 per cent from 45.9 per cent in 2021 — voters came back in order to leave the ruling party. The commission is still expected to declare the official outcome and seat allocations for the 257 councils on Sunday, 8 November; after that, councils have fourteen days to elect mayors and speakers, which is where the real contest — hundreds of local bargains, including in Johannesburg, Tshwane and eThekwini — now begins. The rand has stayed calm while the arithmetic sorts itself out.

Latest · Africa · Kenya — 191 Murders Proven7 November 2026

200 Years for the Shakahola Preacher: Mackenzie Jailed over 190 Children Starved on His Orders

Kenya’s High Court in Mombasa closed the central chapter of the Shakahola horror on Friday. Justice Diana Kavedza sentenced cult leader Paul Nthenge Mackenzie to 200 years in prison — 100 years for 183 murders committed through starvation, and a further consecutive 100 years for eight murders by direct homicide, including suffocation, strangulation and blunt-force trauma — after his conviction for the deaths of 190 children and one adult between January 2021 and September 2023. The court found Mackenzie, founder of Good News International Church, had radicalised his followers with an apocalyptic teaching that starving themselves and their children would let them meet Jesus before the end of the world, and placed him at the centre of the killings as the settlement’s architect. Prosecutors had sought the death penalty; the judge declined, noting executions have not been carried out in Kenya since 1987. Twenty-eight co-accused were jailed alongside him, his deputy receiving a cumulative 160 years and others terms from 10 to 160 years, with 14 days to appeal. The trial covered only part of the catastrophe: 448 bodies were ultimately recovered from the forest near Malindi after the graves were discovered in April 2023, and Mackenzie is also accused over 52 further deaths at a second site, a suspected toll of around 500.

Latest · Africa · Tanzania — Treason Charge Fails7 November 2026

Cleared of Treason, Jailed for Words: Lissu Gets Four Years for Incitement after 577 Days Inside

Tanzania’s most watched trial ended in a split verdict on Friday. A three-judge High Court panel in Dar es Salaam, led by Justice Dunstan Ndunguru, acquitted opposition leader Tundu Lissu of treason — a charge carrying a possible death sentence — ruling that prosecutors had failed to prove he intended to intimidate the government. The same panel then convicted the CHADEMA chairman of the lesser offence of incitement, holding that his “No Reforms, No Election” campaign and calls to disrupt the October 2025 vote crossed the legal line, and sentenced him to four years in prison. Lissu, arrested on 9 April 2025 after a rally in Mbinga and charged the next day over social-media posts urging an election boycott, has now spent 577 days in detention; he called the treason case a political fabrication and has leave to appeal, and his party says it will appeal and seek bail in the meantime. Amnesty International condemned the outcome as the product of a deeply flawed process and demanded his release, and the Pan-African Solidarity Network called for an independent international investigation into alleged abuses around the 2025 election. The four-year term means Tanzania’s main opposition figure remains behind bars despite beating the charge that could have hanged him.

Latest · Middle East · Israel — 67 Endorsements to 467 November 2026

67 to 46: Eisenkot Has the Numbers to Replace Netanyahu — and a Coalition Puzzle to Solve First

Israel’s post-election consultations have produced a frontrunner. Former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, closed the week with the backing of 67 members of the 120-seat Knesset to form the next government, against 46 recommendations for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu — whose bloc won 53 seats on 27 October but shrank in the telling after the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism, holding seven seats, refused to endorse anyone. On Friday Eisenkot laid out a four-point basis for what he calls a national emergency government: recognition of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, fidelity to the values of the Declaration of Independence, mandatory military, national or civilian service for all, and recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation whose military and governing power must be dismantled. He signalled he would not rule out a minority government and warned that anyone blocking a government for personal reasons “will not be forgiven”. The arithmetic underneath is delicate: only 48 of his 67 backers come from the Jewish-led change bloc of Yashar, Together, the Democrats and Yisrael Beiteinu, with the rest from Ra’am and the Joint List, whose leaders back him for the mandate without committing to sit in his coalition. President Isaac Herzog is expected to assign the mandate on Sunday, and the new Knesset is sworn in on 10 November.

Eleven Ships in a Day: Hormuz Runs at a Tenth of Normal as Trump Weighs Striking Iran Again

The world’s most important oil chokepoint ended the week barely moving. Tracking data counted 11 AIS-visible commercial crossings of the Strait of Hormuz on Friday — three inbound, eight outbound, four of them tankers — against a seven-day average of 9.1 a day, roughly 11 per cent of the pre-crisis norm of about 85, with the waterway officially rated “restricted” and the maritime threat level severe. Over the week just 64 ships crossed, and none of them an LNG or LPG carrier. Diplomacy and escalation are running in parallel. Iran has sent Washington a nuclear proposal through Qatar, but insists it will only be discussed once the United States meets its seven demands for reopening the strait; Oman and Iran have discussed practical arrangements for safe navigation, and Russia’s President Putin met Iran’s security chief Mohsen Rezaei in Moscow. Against that, President Trump says he will soon decide whether to resume military action now the midterms are over and has warned the US may have to hit Iran harder; Tehran’s army spokesman answered that renewed attacks would bring retaliation against American assets worldwide. The USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier group is reported en route to join two others in the region by mid-November, Iraq has stepped up radiation monitoring along the Iranian border, and the confirmed death of a seafarer aboard the tanker Velos Sapphire stands as the week’s human cost.

“Very Close to Settling”: Trump’s Big Claim, Melania’s Letters, and a Kyiv Where Every Power Plant Is Hit

The Ukraine endgame has a date and a venue. The first US-mediated trilateral talks between Russia, Ukraine and the United States since February are set for 10–11 November in Abu Dhabi, and President Trump — after a private White House meeting with Vladimir Putin’s envoy Kirill Dmitriev — told reporters “I think we’re going to be very close to settling Russia-Ukraine”, promising a “big, beautiful agreement” that might involve all of Europe. The meeting grew out of an event hosted by First Lady Melania Trump marking the six-day Oleshky ceasefire she helped broker in October; she sent both delegations home with personal letters to Putin and to President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and called for something “more enduring than a temporary ceasefire”. Kyiv is calibrating carefully: Zelenskyy, who held a detailed 90-minute call with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, says Ukraine must adapt to the new balance in Washington after the Democrats took Congress, and is pressing for air defences and a new satellite-access contract. The ground truth remains grim — Zelenskyy says every Ukrainian power plant has been damaged by Russian strikes, Kyiv’s mayor and president traded blame on Friday over blackouts and water outages in the capital, Ukrainian forces struck an oil refinery inside Russia, and Polish foreign minister Radosław Sikorski insisted a ceasefire must come before any settlement talks begin.

Latest · Global Affairs · Defence — Ratified 279–119 in Ankara7 November 2026

An Attack on One Is an Attack on All: Türkiye Ratifies the Mecca Defence Pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan

A new mutual-defence architecture in the Muslim world cleared a decisive hurdle on Friday. Türkiye’s parliament approved the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan by 279 votes to 119, completing Ankara’s ratification of a pact under which an external armed attack on any one of the three countries is treated as an attack on all. The vote binds together NATO’s second-largest military, the Gulf’s biggest economy and a nuclear-armed South Asian state at a moment when the nine-month Middle East war, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the US naval blockade of Iran have pushed every regional capital to hedge its security bets. For Pakistan, the pact formalises a defence relationship with Riyadh that has deepened through the war; for Saudi Arabia, it adds two substantial militaries to its deterrent posture beyond its traditional reliance on Washington; for Türkiye, it extends its strategic footprint from the Black Sea to the Arabian Sea. What the pact will mean in practice — joint planning, basing, procurement or only a political guarantee — now depends on the three governments’ implementing steps, but the parliamentary arithmetic in Ankara leaves no doubt about the intent: the agreement is meant to be read in Tehran, and in every other capital watching the Hormuz crisis.

Latest · Americas · Grenada — 14 of 15 Seats7 November 2026

A Caribbean Landslide: Dickon Mitchell’s NDC Takes 14 of Grenada’s 15 Seats

The week’s quietest election produced one of its most emphatic results. Grenada’s ruling National Democratic Congress, led by Prime Minister Dickon Mitchell, won 14 of the 15 seats in the House of Representatives in Thursday’s general election, according to results released by the Parliamentary Elections Office on Thursday evening — a near-sweep that leaves the opposition holding a single seat in the new parliament. The outcome hands Mitchell, who first took office in 2022 promising economic renewal for the island nation of about 117,000 people, an overwhelming mandate for a second term, with the parliamentary arithmetic to pass legislation essentially unopposed. In a week when voters on three continents punished incumbents — in the US midterms, in South Africa’s municipalities and in Israel’s Knesset — Grenada ran the other way, consolidating power behind the government it already had. Attention in St George’s now turns to the cabinet Mitchell assembles and to how a one-seat opposition performs the scrutiny role that Grenadian politics, like everywhere else, still needs done.
