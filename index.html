Brazil Counts Its Runoff: Lula Holds a Slim Early Lead Over Bolsonaro, With the Race Far From Called

Voting has ended in Brazil's presidential runoff and the count is under way, with President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva narrowly ahead of Senator Flávio Bolsonaro on the first returns. With 3.1% of polling sections reported by 7:24 p.m. Brasília time, Lula had 51.3% of valid votes — 1,977,058 ballots — against 48.7%, or 1,873,629, for Bolsonaro, a gap of about 103,000 votes. The incumbent's advantage has already narrowed sharply from the electoral court's first bulletin, which put him above 57% on a tiny fraction of the count, and no winner had been declared. Final opinion polls pointed the other way: both Datafolha and Genial/Quaest put Bolsonaro on 51% of valid votes to Lula's 49%, a statistical tie. Lula, who turns 81 this week, is seeking an unprecedented fourth term; Bolsonaro, 45, the eldest son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro, finished first in the October 4 opening round by 47.03% to 45.16%. No candidate who has trailed in a Brazilian first round since 1989 has gone on to win the runoff. Governors were also being decided in five states and the Federal District.

Latest · Europe · France25 October 2026

French Unions Call Fresh Protests and a November 5 Strike as Macron's Room to Concede Shrinks

France's protest movement is not standing down. Unions and protest groups on Sunday called for another round of demonstrations next Saturday, October 31, and a nationwide strike on November 5, determined to keep pressure on the government after a vast security operation contained Saturday's rallies. The Interior Ministry put turnout at 410,000 nationwide, with 70,000 police deployed; 1,314 people were arrested — half the previous weekend's figure — and about 50 officers were injured, down from more than 1,000 a week earlier, while looting collapsed from 1,522 businesses hit on October 17 to just 24. “We see an aggregation of anger,” said Murielle Guilbert, co-leader of the Solidaires union federation, arguing that grievances over wages, living costs and public services now reach far beyond the students who first mobilised last month; the Yellow Vests have also called sit-ins outside town halls and the National Assembly. The government has opened talks with unions and student representatives, but protest leaders call its offers inadequate, and police unions warn they lack the staff to sustain Saturday's level of deployment for long. With strained public finances, a term ending next April and no parliamentary majority, President Emmanuel Macron has little room to buy calm as he could in 2019.

Israel Votes Tomorrow Deadlocked: Final Polls Put Eisenkot's Bloc Ahead, but Nobody Near a Majority

Israel goes to the polls on Tuesday in its first general election since the October 7, 2023 attack, and the final surveys describe a country split down the middle. The last polls legally permitted before election day, published Friday by the three main television channels, all showed former military chief Gadi Eisenkot's bloc ahead of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's coalition by two to six seats — Channel 12 projected 56 seats to 50, Channel 13 gave 54 to 50, and Kan 52 to 50 — with neither camp close to the 61 seats needed to govern in the 120-member Knesset. Eisenkot's Yashar party, founded barely a year ago, is forecast to be the largest single faction with 22–23 seats, ahead of Netanyahu's Likud on 19–21, while the Together list of former premiers Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid sits on 14–15. Arab-majority parties, polling at 14–16 seats, could hold the balance in the coalition bargaining that follows. Netanyahu, Israel's longest-serving prime minister, campaigns on security and carries an endorsement from Donald Trump, but the campaign's final days were shaken by Friday's checkpoint attack near Hebron, in which two soldiers were killed; all West Bank crossings will be closed on election day.

Flights Resume at Riyadh Airport After a Two-Week Closure, but the Houthis Say It Is Still a Target

Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport reopened on Sunday after two weeks shut by Houthi attacks, splitting its terminals between domestic flights and international services by national carriers — even as explosions rattled the Saudi capital in the early hours following a text-message warning of a hostile threat. Flight-tracking data showed only a handful of departures, with the vast majority of services still cancelled, and the Houthis' military spokesman Yahya Saree insisted the airport remains “unsafe” and a military target. The closure has been a heavy blow to the kingdom: an attack on the airport this month killed 13 people and wounded more than 300, the deadliest strike in Saudi Arabia in over a decade; the flagship Future Investment Initiative conference has been postponed until 2027, and the Dakar Rally has joined the list of events cancelled, postponed or moved. Foreign carriers are in no hurry back — India's airlines have suspended Saudi routes in stages running to November 7 — while residents who have spent a fortnight scrambling for alternative ways in and out of the country weigh whether the reopening will hold.

Latest · Europe · Ukraine25 October 2026

Starlink Speeds Restored in Ukraine After a Week of Throttling That Musk Admits Was His Call

Ukraine's Starlink and Starshield satellite links are running at full speed again after more than a week of restrictions that hampered front-line operations, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has confirmed. The limits, which cut terminals off at speeds above roughly 160 km/h, had forced the cancellation of some operations and blunted Ukraine's ability to intercept Russian attack drones; Kyiv's defence ministry says the connection is now stable above 400 km/h. “The speeds are now where they need to be,” Zelenskyy wrote, saying restoration began overnight into Saturday. Hours later, Elon Musk acknowledged that his company SpaceX had throttled the civilian Starlink service, accusing Ukraine's military of violating its terms by using it “for offensive drone strikes to kill people” and saying the loophole had been closed. He maintained that Starshield, the military-grade system, is controlled by the US government, and the Wall Street Journal has reported that President Donald Trump personally phoned Musk to ask him to lift the restrictions. The episode has further strained Kyiv's relations with Washington, already tense over Trump's deal to buy Russian diesel while pressing Ukraine to stop striking Russian refineries.

Twelve Injured, Six of Them Children, in Shooting Near Florida A&M Stadium on Homecoming Night

Twelve people were injured, six of them children, when gunfire broke out on Saturday night near Florida A&M University's Bragg Memorial Stadium in Tallahassee, as the city celebrated the university's homecoming weekend. Police said a fight between a small number of people escalated into shooting just before 8 p.m. near Perry Street, shortly after a football game. Eleven of the victims suffered gunshot wounds and a twelfth was injured; at least three were in serious condition while the other nine were stable. The youngest of those shot was 11 years old. No arrests had been made by Sunday, and the police violent crimes unit is investigating. “It is especially heartbreaking to know that children are among those injured,” said Police Chief Lawrence Revell. The university said the shooting happened off campus and that the campus itself remained safe, adding that the safety of its students, faculty and staff was its highest priority.

Trump Unveils Audi's $4.2 Billion First US Plant in Georgia, Ten Days Before the Midterms

President Donald Trump has used a campaign rally in Perry, Georgia, to announce that Audi will invest $4.2 billion in its first American factory, presenting the deal as proof his tariffs are pulling manufacturing back to the United States. The Volkswagen-owned brand confirmed it will build the plant near Macon with capacity for up to 200,000 vehicles a year — its largest, most premium SUVs — creating about 4,500 jobs. “Without tariffs, they wouldn't be coming in,” Trump told the crowd, calling it the largest investment in modern US auto history. Audi chief executive Gernot Döllner, who joined him on stage, described it as “a long-term investment, because we believe in the American market.” The timelines differ, however: Trump said construction would begin next month, while Audi says work starts in 2027 with production in the early 2030s. The announcement lands in a battleground state where Republicans are trying to unseat Democratic Senator Jon Ossoff, and where their candidate trails in polls ahead of the November 3 midterms; parent company Volkswagen, meanwhile, is in the midst of a restructuring that unions agreed in September could cut up to 100,000 jobs by 2030.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · India26 October 2026

Cyclone Arnab Forms Over the Bay of Bengal, With a 'Very Severe' Landfall Due on Wednesday

A depression over the Bay of Bengal is expected to become Cyclone Arnab on Monday morning and keep strengthening — to a severe cyclonic storm by Tuesday morning and a very severe one before it crosses India's Andhra Pradesh coast between Bapatla and Visakhapatnam on Wednesday forenoon, October 28, the India Meteorological Department says. Winds at landfall are forecast at 115–125 km/h, gusting to 140 km/h. Neighbouring Odisha, which expects the heaviest rain rather than the strongest winds, has cancelled leave for disaster personnel and placed 225 rescue teams on high alert, with a red warning for extremely heavy rainfall from Tuesday across five southern districts: Ganjam, Gajapati, Rayagada, Koraput and Malkangiri. Evacuations have been stepped up in vulnerable coastal and low-lying areas, and schools and colleges in the five districts will stay shut on October 28 and 29. Bhubaneswar meteorological centre director Manorama Mohanty said Odisha's rainfall impact “will be more significant” than its wind impact. The name Arnab, meaning “ocean” in Bengali, was contributed by Bangladesh.

Latest · Africa · DR Congo24 October 2026

Congo Launches a Landmark Vaccine Trial as the Ebola Toll Passes 4,600 in Its Largest-Ever Outbreak

The Democratic Republic of Congo has begun a major clinical trial of Ebola vaccines in the eastern city of Bunia, testing candidate shots against the Bundibugyo strain in the middle of the largest outbreak the country has ever recorded. The Tumaini trial, a Phase 2/3 study using ring vaccination around confirmed cases, starts with Ervebo — a vaccine licensed against the Zaire strain of Ebola but never proven against Bundibugyo — with two purpose-built candidates, one from Moderna and one from Oxford University with the Serum Institute of India, expected to join shortly. “The launch of the Tumaini trial marks a critical step towards establishing whether candidate vaccines can effectively protect people against the Bundibugyo virus,” said Chikwe Ihekweazu, head of the WHO's Health Emergencies Programme. Congolese health authorities put confirmed cases at 9,704 as of October 20, with 4,687 deaths — a fatality rate of 48.3%. The epidemic, declared on May 15 and made a global health emergency two days later, is now the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record, and cases have also been confirmed in Uganda, Kenya, Nigeria and France.

Latest · Americas · Cuba25 October 2026

Twenty-Hour Blackouts: Cuba Tells the UN the US Blockade Now Costs It More Than $7 Billion a Year

Cuban households are enduring average power cuts of more than 20 hours a day as the island's energy crisis deepens, Havana says in its latest annual report on the impact of the United States embargo, which it estimates cost the economy more than US$7 billion between March 2025 and February 2026 — a 7% rise on the previous period, with cumulative losses in the trillions. Fuel shortages have hit hospitals, water pumping and food distribution, and officials say faltering water supplies now affect nearly 29% of Cuba's 9.4 million people; in the provinces, blackouts have at times stretched beyond 60 hours. Washington extended the embargo for another year in August, with President Donald Trump calling Cuba an “extraordinary threat” to US national security — a characterisation rejected in an open letter this weekend by former Caribbean leaders, who wrote that “Cuba is not a threat to any nation or any person on planet Earth.” Cuba's foreign minister Bruno Rodríguez has called the policy “genocidal”; the US has long defended sanctions by pointing to Cuba's political system and human rights record, and attributes the island's hardships to mismanagement in Havana.
