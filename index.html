Latest · Asia-Pacific · New Zealand — 3,500 Votes Apart7 November 2026

3,500 Votes Apart, Weeks to Wait: New Zealand’s Election Ends Without a Winner

New Zealand has voted and nobody has won — at least not yet. With about 95 per cent of ballots counted on Saturday night, Prime Minister Christopher Luxon’s centre-right National Party and Chris Hipkins’ Labour were locked just below 30 per cent each, separated by roughly 3,500 votes, in a campaign dominated by household costs and a sluggish economy. Preliminary seat maths gives the governing three-party bloc of National, ACT and New Zealand First 67 seats in an expanded parliament of about 130 to 131 members — a majority of one or two — while the Labour, Green and Te Pāti Māori grouping sits well short. The surprise of the night is the centrist Opportunity Party, which cleared the 5 per cent threshold for the first time and is projected to take seven seats, a position that could make it a kingmaker if the special votes shift the balance. Luxon told supporters in Auckland that National remained the largest party with a pathway back to office, but conceded the result was too close to call and said he would lead a caretaker government in the meantime. Roughly half a million to 700,000 special votes — cast abroad, while travelling or outside a home electorate — are still to be counted, and they have historically leaned left. The Electoral Commission will not declare the final result until 27 November, leaving three weeks of limbo and, very likely, hard coalition bargaining after it.

Latest · Middle East · Israel — Decision on Sunday7 November 2026

67 Back Eisenkot, 46 Back Netanyahu: Herzog Holds Israel’s Next Government in One Decision

Israel’s next government now turns on a single announcement due on Sunday. President Isaac Herzog has finished consulting party leaders after the inconclusive 27 October election and says he will task one lawmaker with forming a coalition — and on the numbers alone, former army chief Gadi Eisenkot starts in front. Parties totalling 67 of the Knesset’s 120 members recommended Eisenkot, the largest such backing since 2015, against 46 for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The catch is where Eisenkot’s majority comes from: his own Jewish-led bloc holds only 48 seats, and the extra 19 belong to Arab-led parties — the Joint List and Ra’am — whose support helped him past 61 but whom he has so far ruled out of government unless conditions are met, including recognising Hamas as a terrorist organisation. Netanyahu’s bloc holds 53 seats, yet even that understates his problem: long-time ally United Torah Judaism, with seven seats, refused to recommend anyone, withholding support over the failure to exempt ultra-Orthodox men from military service — a refusal that also leaves those seven seats as Eisenkot’s cleanest route to exactly 61 if he can win them over. Herzog, who rejected Likud claims of election fraud when he certified the results, warned that mutual vetoes inside and between the blocs make the arithmetic look clearer than the politics. Whoever he names will have 28 days, extendable by 14, to assemble a government — or Israel heads toward yet another election.

Latest · Africa · Kenya — Shakahola Sentencing7 November 2026

200 Years for 191 Murders: Kenya Jails the Preacher Who Told Followers to Starve to Reach Heaven

A court in Mombasa has closed the main trial in one of the deadliest cult cases of modern times. Paul Mackenzie, who led the Good News International Church from a forest settlement at Shakahola near Kenya’s Indian Ocean coast, was sentenced on Friday to 200 years in prison — 100 years for murder by starvation and a further 100 for direct homicide, to run consecutively in effect — after being convicted last month alongside 28 associates of murdering 190 children and one adult. Prosecutors said Mackenzie instructed followers to fast to death, children included, so they would reach heaven before the world ended; some victims, the court heard, were buried alive or dumped in latrines. His 28 co-defendants received terms ranging from 10 to 160 years, and his lawyer said an appeal would follow. The trial covered only part of the horror uncovered from April 2023, when police began finding shallow graves in the forest: the Kenya National Commission on Human Rights puts the number of bodies recovered there at 448, and Mackenzie faces separate proceedings over 52 further deaths at a village some 30 kilometres away, taking the suspected toll to about 500. Prosecutors had asked for the death penalty; the judge declined, saying it would remove any possibility of rehabilitation. For families such as that of Stephen Mwiti, who lost six children, the sentence answers the criminal question — but not how a preacher already known to authorities since 2017, whose Malindi church was shut in 2019, was able to move into the forest and carry on.

Latest · Global Affairs · Markets — Worst Week Since April 20257 November 2026

$3 Trillion Gone on Thursday, a Bounce on Friday: the Week the AI Trade Broke

Wall Street has ended its worst week since April 2025, and the damage was done in a single session. After Democrats swept both chambers in Tuesday’s midterms, investors spent Thursday dumping artificial-intelligence stocks on fears of tighter regulation of data centres, chips and AI development — a sell-off that erased roughly $3 trillion in market value in one day, sent the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down more than 12 per cent in its worst session in years, and knocked 9 per cent off Nvidia and more than 17 per cent off AMD. Friday brought a partial recovery rather than a reversal: the S&P 500 rose about 0.3 per cent to close near 7,376, the Nasdaq gained about 1.1 per cent to around 24,579 and chip stocks rebounded 4.2 per cent, while the Dow slipped 0.46 per cent. For the week, however, the S&P was down 2.7 per cent and the Nasdaq 3.4 per cent, and the shock travelled — Japan’s Nikkei and South Korea’s Kospi each fell about 6 per cent, with trading in Seoul briefly halted, and Europe’s STOXX 600 lost 1 per cent. Oil told the opposite political story in the same week: Brent settled Friday at $98.32 a barrel, down roughly 16 to 17 per cent over five sessions — its steepest weekly fall since the pandemic — as traders bet the election result makes de-escalation in the Iran war more likely. Gold was little changed near $4,066 an ounce. Two bets are now embedded in prices at once: that Washington will regulate AI harder, and that it will fight Iran less.

Strikes, Sanctions and a $20 Million Reward: Washington Presses Iran While Qatar Carries the Proposals

Even as American aircraft struck Iranian air defences on Qeshm Island, the diplomatic and financial tracks around Iran moved quickly this week — in opposite directions. Qatar is shuttling proposals between Washington and Tehran focused on the nuclear programme, and officials say Iran is reviewing them, with talk of a short two-day “bridge” arrangement to rebuild confidence before any wider talks. Tehran, on the reported accounts, is not ready to negotiate directly until its ceasefire conditions are addressed: lifting the blockade on Iranian ports, easing sanctions, releasing frozen assets and compensation for war damage. A senior Iranian official has also said Tehran sent its own nuclear proposal to the United States via Qatar. At the same time, Washington widened the pressure. The State Department’s Rewards for Justice programme is offering up to $20 million, plus possible relocation, for information on Iran’s nuclear facilities, equipment and personnel, including scientists — citing international inspectors’ concerns and Iran’s stockpile of uranium enriched to 60 per cent. Separately, the Treasury sanctioned eight Canada-based money-services businesses, among them firms trading as Million Exchange, Amin Exchange, Farah Exchange and Darvish Exchange, accusing them of converting Iranian rials and other currencies into digital assets for sanctioned Iranian entities, including the Revolutionary Guard; Canada’s financial-intelligence agency, FINTRAC, coordinated on the action. President Trump says contacts with Tehran are going “very well”. The gap between that optimism and Tehran’s preconditions is, for now, the negotiation itself.

“Sham” or Showdown Next Week? Hegseth Fights an Impeachment Push as Rubio Denies He Is Leaving

The midterm backlash inside Washington has landed on two of President Trump’s most senior officials — and the White House spent Friday insisting neither is going anywhere. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth faces a threat from Republican Representative Thomas Massie to trigger an impeachment resolution as early as Monday or Tuesday, when lawmakers return. Massie’s articles accuse Hegseth of, among other things, conducting military operations against Iran without congressional authorisation, and Massie has argued publicly that Trump should replace him now, while a Republican Senate could still confirm a successor, rather than wait for the Democratic Congress that takes over in January. Hegseth answered with a video statement defending the Iran war as courageous and historic, calling the impeachment effort a sham and a politics of personal destruction, and vowing not to back down; Trump has said he intends to keep him. Alongside that fight, Secretary of State Marco Rubio moved to kill speculation that he might quit to prepare a 2028 presidential run. Rubio told The New York Times that serving out Trump’s term had always been his plan, said he loved the work, and joked, when asked about leaving his job, “Which one?” — a nod to his multiple roles. The White House and State Department both labelled the departure reports fake news. The denials settle nothing about January, when a hostile Congress will hold the subpoena power — and when both men’s wars, foreign and political, get harder.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · India — AQI 5817 November 2026

581 on the Index, Double the Next City: Delhi Is the World’s Most Polluted Major City on Diwali Eve

Delhi woke on Saturday to the worst air of any major city on earth, on the worst possible weekend. The Swiss monitoring group IQAir placed the Indian capital first in its live global ranking at 10:35 a.m., with a reading of 581 on the US air-quality index — a level classed as hazardous for the whole population, and more than double the 285 recorded in second-placed Lahore, Pakistan. India’s own Central Pollution Control Board, which uses a different scale and method, put Delhi at 363 at 10 a.m., in its “very poor” band; across the city’s 59 reporting stations, 16 recorded severe air and 29 very poor, with a peak of 452 near IIT Delhi. Local reporting says Friday’s air was already the worst before Diwali in eight years, and forecasters expect conditions to stay severe through 8 and 9 November, once firecrackers for the Hindu festival of lights add to crop-residue smoke, traffic fumes and stagnant winter air. Authorities responded with curbs that have become an annual ritual: primary classes shifted to hybrid schooling, offices urged toward working from home, and visibility cut across districts such as Anand Vihar as a thick smog settled over the capital. Kolkata ranked third globally in the same IQAir snapshot at 198 and Mumbai tenth at 142 — a reminder that on Diwali eve, Delhi’s emergency is the sharp end of a regional one.

Latest · Americas · Haiti — 2,000 Salvadoran Troops7 November 2026

2,000 Troops In, Four Men Still Missing: El Salvador’s Haiti Intervention Tests Who Is in Charge

More than 2,000 Salvadoran soldiers are now operating in Haiti, in a deployment that has doubled the foreign force there in little over a week — and raised a basic question nobody has fully answered: under whose command? President Nayib Bukele ordered the surge after four Salvadoran aviation technicians, supporting medical evacuations with the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force, disappeared in an ambush on 18 October in Croix-des-Bouquets, outside Port-au-Prince. Haitian police this week found the burned-out vehicle the men had used, in the Santo neighbourhood, but not the men. A joint operation on 2 November swept the Cul-de-Sac Plain without finding them. Bukele has posted videos of heavily armed patrols and helicopter fire, and challenged the gangs directly online. Wilson Joseph, the US-wanted leader of the 400 Mawozo gang, has written to the UN Secretary-General seeking dialogue and mediation — and claimed, without evidence, that the four are dead; Haiti’s government says they were abducted and El Salvador says they are alive. The UN has acknowledged receiving the letter while stressing that acknowledgement is not endorsement, and that only about 200 of the Salvadorans fall under the Gang Suppression Force’s mandate. Haiti’s Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé insists foreign forces operate under Haitian authority. Meanwhile the country, where gangs control most of the capital, has set its first general elections in over a decade for 18 July 2027 — a date that will mean little if the security question is still being fought over street by street.

Latest · Global Affairs · Food Security — Exports Down 29%7 November 2026

Rotting in Silos While Buyers Go Hungry: Ukraine’s Trapped Grain Links Two Wars to One Food Crisis

Two distant blockades are converging on the same dinner tables. Ukraine, long one of the world’s cheapest and most reliable grain suppliers to the Middle East and North Africa, had exported 29 per cent less by 30 October than at the same point a year earlier, according to its agriculture ministry — a season that began ahead of last year’s pace before Russian strikes on ports around Odesa from August choked shipments. Analysts warn that more than nine million tonnes of Ukrainian grain, oilseeds and meal could be left without adequate storage by November, rotting while it is needed elsewhere. The Black Sea has become a battlefield in its own right: maritime-risk firm Ambrey counts 317 merchant and other vessels hit by the two sides between May and October, more than four times the 74 of the previous six months. At the other end of the supply chain, attacks around the Strait of Hormuz are disrupting the food, fertiliser and energy flows the same importing countries depend on. The human exposure is already measured: in Yemen, 47 per cent of people in government-held areas face high levels of acute food insecurity, with 1.8 million expected at emergency level between October and December, while in Gaza more than 1.4 million people are projected at crisis level or worse into December. UN food officials make the structural point plainly — countries that buy Ukrainian wheat also ship through Gulf routes, so a missile in either sea lands, eventually, in the price and availability of bread in both regions.

Latest · Middle East · Gulf — Ships Over the Cables7 November 2026

Drifting With No Commercial Reason to Stop: Iran-Linked Ships Tracked Over the Gulf’s Internet Cables

The next vulnerability in the Gulf war may be under the water rather than on it. Maritime-intelligence firm Windward, in research reported by the Financial Times, tracked three sanctioned Iran-linked tankers and an Iranian fishing vessel slowing, looping and drifting during October directly above major undersea telecommunications cables in and near the Strait of Hormuz — movements the firm says had no obvious commercial explanation. The stakes are unusually concentrated: six active cables cross the contested strait linking the Middle East to Europe, Africa and Asia, two more are under construction, and Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar depend entirely on cables through Hormuz for their external connectivity. One of the corridors the vessels lingered over includes the 2Africa system, backed by a consortium including Meta, whose expansion work has already been paused because cable-laying and repair ships cannot safely operate in the region since the war began in February. No cable has been reported cut, and the reporting stops short of proving intent — loitering is a signal, not an attack. But in a conflict that has already reached tankers, ports, air defences and, this week, a seafarer’s life aboard the Velos Sapphire, the implication security analysts draw is straightforward: if strikes resume at scale, the infrastructure that carries the region’s banking, government and everyday internet traffic is charted, reachable and — unlike a pipeline — very hard to guard.

Latest · Middle East · Gaza — After the Floods7 November 2026

“A Stain on the Conscience”: After the Tents Flooded, Gaza’s Argument Moves to Who Failed to Prevent It

A day after Gaza’s first winter storm flooded displacement camps across the territory, the argument has shifted from the weather to responsibility. Hamas spokesman Hazem Qassem said on Saturday that the catastrophe facing displaced families — hundreds of tents inundated as heavy rain compounded shortages, destruction and siege — shames not only Israel but the international bodies charged with relief, naming the so-called Board of Peace and its representative Nikolay Mladenov, whom he accused of failing to ensure aid deliveries and of silence over the blockade of more than two million people. He called on the Arab League to act urgently, in line with the purposes for which it was founded. The political charge lands on top of a physical emergency that needs no rhetoric: families who lost homes to war spent the storm in tents and makeshift shelters among rubble, in districts from Jabalia in the north to Khan Younis in the south, with sewage, mud and standing water now adding disease risk to exposure. Gaza’s health authorities put the Palestinian death toll since October 2023 at 74,618 — a figure that frames why so many people remain displaced in the first place, and why a rainstorm can function as a mass-casualty risk. Separately, medical sources reported that two Palestinians died on Friday from wounds sustained in earlier Israeli attacks on Gaza City, including a medical student at the Islamic University and a resident of al-Shati refugee camp, in what Palestinian reporting describes as continuing violations of a fragile ceasefire. Winter has barely begun; the tents, and the diplomacy meant to replace them with shelter, are both running out of time.

Latest · Europe · France — 2027 Platform Published7 November 2026

Gaza at the Centre, Ukraine at the Margins: Mélenchon Rewrites the French Left’s Foreign Policy for 2027

France’s radical left has published the foreign-policy core of its 2027 presidential platform, and its ordering of the world is deliberate. In the updated programme L’Avenir en commun, released on Friday, Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s La France Insoumise places the war in Gaza and the colonisation of the West Bank at the centre of its international outlook — accusing the French authorities of having allowed what it calls a genocide in Gaza, and criticising President Emmanuel Macron’s policy in strong terms — while condemning the wars against Lebanon and Iran in the same language it uses for aggression against Ukraine. Ukraine itself, by contrast, is handled briefly: the programme speaks of building a diplomatic framework toward a ceasefire but does not settle the movement’s long-ambiguous position on military support for Kyiv, and the question of arms is simply not addressed. Mélenchon also reaffirms his intention to take France out of NATO. The emphasis is not new — Gaza already dominated the party’s 2024 European election campaign — but its formal place at the head of a presidential platform sharpens the contrast inside French politics ahead of 2027: a candidate asking voters to judge foreign policy primarily through the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, at a moment when most of his rivals ask them to judge it through Russia. Whether that ordering is a liability or a mobilising strength will be one of the defining arguments of the campaign.
