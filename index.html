Latest · Americas · Paraguay — Peña Under House Arrest7 November 2026

Out and Under House Arrest: Alliana Sworn In as Peña Faces a Judge on Saturday

Paraguay’s presidency changed hands on Friday night, and its former holder spent the night confined to his home. After the Senate voted to remove Santiago Peña from office — all 44 senators present backed the verdict, according to reports of the vote — Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in as president to serve out the term to August 2028. Prosecutors then ordered Peña held under house arrest on suspicion of breach of trust, an offence carrying up to five years in prison, or ten in serious cases, and he is due before the economic crimes unit on Saturday. The impeachment strips him of the parliamentary immunity a lifetime Senate seat would have carried, which is precisely why Congress refused his resignation and convicted him instead. The attorney general’s office has three criminal cases open over the Ueno Bank collapse, which froze the accounts of nearly half the country’s more than six million people, and an arrest order also names Carlos Carvallo, who ran the finance company that became Ueno before Peña appointed him central bank chief in 2023. Lawmakers accused the ousted president of steering hundreds of millions of dollars in public funds into the bank while regulators overlooked risky insider lending. Peña is only the second Paraguayan president removed by impeachment since democracy returned in 1989.

Latest · Americas · Washington — Chief of Staff Change7 November 2026

Miller In, Wiles Out: Trump Answers a Midterm Rout by Promoting His Immigration Hardliner

The first big personnel move of the post-midterm White House landed on Friday. President Donald Trump announced that Stephen Miller, his deputy chief of staff for policy and the architect of the administration’s hardline immigration agenda, will become chief of staff on Monday, 9 November, replacing Susie Wiles, who submitted her resignation on Thursday evening and will move to Trump’s political operation. Wiles, 69, was the first woman ever to hold the job and the longest-serving chief of staff of either Trump term; she had run his three campaigns and stayed in post through months of breast-cancer treatment after a diagnosis she made public in March. The change follows days of Republican recrimination after the party lost both the House and the Senate, with MAGA figures including Steve Bannon blaming Wiles for the strategy. Miller, 41, has steadily widened his remit far beyond immigration into domestic policy and foreign affairs, and Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer read the promotion as proof that “Trump has learned nothing from his historic defeat”. Miller called the appointment “the privilege of my lifetime”. The signal is plain: rather than pivot toward the incoming Democratic Congress, the White House is doubling down on the agenda voters just rejected.

“Doing Very Well with Iran” — and a Drone Downed over Qeshm in the Same News Cycle

The Iran file is running on two tracks at once. President Trump told reporters the United States is talking to Tehran’s representatives and is “doing very well with Iran”, after a phone call with Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, whose country is mediating. Hours later, Iran’s army said its air defences had intercepted and destroyed an American MQ-9 Reaper drone over Qeshm Island, at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz, after Iranian media reported two projectiles striking the island; US Central Command described the situation around Qeshm as “relatively calm”, and the Revolutionary Guard separately claimed three tankers had been struck in the strait. The diplomacy underneath remains stuck on the same point: Tehran’s seven conditions for reopening Hormuz — including sanctions relief, release of frozen funds and an end to the US naval blockade — which a senior Iranian security official has already dismissed Washington’s reply to as “unconvincing”. Trump has also warned the US may have to hit Iran harder now the midterms are over, and three carrier groups are expected in the region by mid-November. For now the strait stays effectively closed, the talks stay technically alive, and each side is demonstrating it can still shoot.

Putin Briefs His Negotiators, Europe Demands a Seat: the Abu Dhabi Table Takes Shape

The delegations for next week’s trilateral talks are now named, and the argument over who else belongs in the room has begun. On Friday President Vladimir Putin convened Russia’s Security Council together with the officials who will travel to Abu Dhabi: the delegation will again be led by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky, joined by Yury Ushakov, deputy foreign ministers Mikhail Galuzin and Sergey Ryabkov and military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner will represent Washington at the 10–11 November meeting — the first trilateral round since February — with an agenda covering post-war security guarantees, de-escalation, a halt to attacks on energy infrastructure, Black Sea arrangements, prisoner exchanges and the return of children. Kyiv is lobbying hard for Europe to be involved before and after the talks: foreign minister Andrii Sybiha made the case at a Baltic council meeting in Warsaw alongside Poland’s Radosław Sikorski and EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov answered that Europeans will not take part, because the format is trilateral. President Zelenskyy says he hopes Abu Dhabi opens a path to a leaders’ meeting with Putin; Germany’s Chancellor Friedrich Merz says he does not yet see Russia negotiating seriously, and analysts at the Atlantic Council warn expectations should be modest — Medinsky’s past rounds produced procedure, not concessions.

Latest · Europe · Aviation — Hoaxes Spread to Newsrooms7 November 2026

From Cockpits to Newsrooms: Europe’s Hoax Bomb Wave Widens for a Fourth Day

Europe’s week of bomb hoaxes broadened on Friday from airliners to the media itself. At least 25 new alerts targeted flights during the day — taking the week’s tally above 40 disrupted services — while emailed threats arrived at newsrooms including Britain’s Sky, Ireland’s RTÉ, Virgin Media, Newstalk and the Irish Independent, and Bulgaria’s Nova TV, several signed by a purported “Mikhail Andropov” under the headline “Message to the West”, and RTÉ staff were evacuated after a warning that a bomb would explode within 45 minutes. In the air, Hungarian fighter jets escorted threatened flights from Brussels to Sofia and from Prague toward Bucharest and Cyprus; a Wizz Air service from Prague to Rome diverted to Venice; Danish police put specialist teams into Copenhagen Airport with baggage re-screening; and Czech police logged threats against nine Prague flights. Every alert so far has proved false and no device has been found, but the pattern — cheap, emailed, cross-border and escalating in target — is exactly what European security officials describe as hybrid disruption, and Eurocontrol says it is monitoring the situation with airlines and airports. No one has credibly claimed responsibility, and governments have not publicly attributed the campaign.

Latest · Americas · Vatican — Day Two in Uruguay7 November 2026

Paysandú at Ten, Casavalle at Five, the Centenario at Seven: Leo’s Longest Day in Uruguay

Pope Leo XIV’s South American journey settles into its rhythm on Saturday with the fullest day of the Uruguay leg. The 71-year-old pontiff, three weeks past lung-cancer surgery, flies in the morning to Paysandú, the river city on the Argentine border, to celebrate Mass at 10 a.m. in Parque París Londres — tens of thousands are expected, many of them pilgrims crossing the international bridge from Colón in Argentina for the first Mass of the tour outside the capital. Back in Montevideo he meets the social community of Casavalle, one of the city’s poorest neighbourhoods, at the parish of Our Lady of Guadalupe, speaks at the Catholic University of Uruguay, and closes the day with a 7 p.m. prayer vigil at the Estadio Centenario, the stadium that hosted the first World Cup final. Friday’s opening address set the tour’s themes: Leo praised Mercosur, peaceful coexistence and care for the environment before Uruguay’s authorities, but also defended life “even that which is still growing in the mother’s womb” and questioned euthanasia — direct words in Latin America’s most secular country, where both are legal. On Sunday he celebrates Mass in Florida before the shrine of the Virgin of the Thirty-Three, Uruguay’s patroness, then flies on to Buenos Aires for the Argentine leg.

Latest · Africa · South Africa — Declaration Due Sunday7 November 2026

Counting Finished, Verdict Pending: South Africa Waits for Sunday’s Declaration — and the Coalition Race

South Africa’s ballots are counted; its result is not yet official. The Electoral Commission says most, if not all, counting is complete, but collation, verification, independent audit and the clearing of objections must finish before the formal declaration, expected on Sunday, 8 November, with a legal deadline of Wednesday. The near-final picture has barely moved: with about 93 to 95 per cent of voting districts in, the ANC sits near 31 per cent — down from 45.6 per cent in 2021 — the Democratic Alliance near 24 to 25 per cent, and Jacob Zuma’s MK Party third; between 108 and 110 of the 257 councils are hung. In Gauteng every municipality except Midvaal, where the DA won a commanding 62 per cent, is hung; Johannesburg, Tshwane and Ekurhuleni are all led by the DA without a majority, and in Mangaung the ANC and DA are neck and neck at roughly 36 per cent each. ActionSA’s Michael Beaumont captured the mood: coalitions have clearly become the order of the day. Once Sunday’s declaration lands, councils have fourteen days to elect mayors and speakers — and the bargaining that will actually decide who governs South Africa’s cities begins in earnest.

Latest · Middle East · Israel — Mandate Due Sunday7 November 2026

67 to 46: Eisenkot Has the Numbers to Replace Netanyahu — and a Coalition Puzzle to Solve First

Israel’s post-election consultations have produced a frontrunner. Former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, closed the week with the backing of 67 members of the 120-seat Knesset to form the next government, against 46 recommendations for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu — whose bloc won 53 seats on 27 October but shrank in the telling after the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism, holding seven seats, refused to endorse anyone. On Friday Eisenkot laid out a four-point basis for what he calls a national emergency government: recognition of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, fidelity to the values of the Declaration of Independence, mandatory military, national or civilian service for all, and recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation whose military and governing power must be dismantled. He signalled he would not rule out a minority government and warned that anyone blocking a government for personal reasons “will not be forgiven”. The arithmetic underneath is delicate: only 48 of his 67 backers come from the Jewish-led change bloc of Yashar, Together, the Democrats and Yisrael Beiteinu, with the rest from Ra’am and the Joint List, whose leaders back him for the mandate without committing to sit in his coalition. President Isaac Herzog is expected to assign the mandate on Sunday, and the new Knesset is sworn in on 10 November.

Latest · Africa · Kenya — 191 Murders Proven7 November 2026

200 Years for the Shakahola Preacher: Mackenzie Jailed over 190 Children Starved on His Orders

Kenya’s High Court in Mombasa closed the central chapter of the Shakahola horror on Friday. Justice Diana Kavedza sentenced cult leader Paul Nthenge Mackenzie to 200 years in prison — 100 years for 183 murders committed through starvation, and a further consecutive 100 years for eight murders by direct homicide, including suffocation, strangulation and blunt-force trauma — after his conviction for the deaths of 190 children and one adult between January 2021 and September 2023. The court found Mackenzie, founder of Good News International Church, had radicalised his followers with an apocalyptic teaching that starving themselves and their children would let them meet Jesus before the end of the world, and placed him at the centre of the killings as the settlement’s architect. Prosecutors had sought the death penalty; the judge declined, noting executions have not been carried out in Kenya since 1987. Twenty-eight co-accused were jailed alongside him, his deputy receiving a cumulative 160 years and others terms from 10 to 160 years, with 14 days to appeal. The trial covered only part of the catastrophe: 448 bodies were ultimately recovered from the forest near Malindi after the graves were discovered in April 2023, and Mackenzie is also accused over 52 further deaths at a second site, a suspected toll of around 500.

Latest · Africa · Tanzania — Treason Charge Fails7 November 2026

Cleared of Treason, Jailed for Words: Lissu Gets Four Years for Incitement after 577 Days Inside

Tanzania’s most watched trial ended in a split verdict on Friday. A three-judge High Court panel in Dar es Salaam, led by Justice Dunstan Ndunguru, acquitted opposition leader Tundu Lissu of treason — a charge carrying a possible death sentence — ruling that prosecutors had failed to prove he intended to intimidate the government. The same panel then convicted the CHADEMA chairman of the lesser offence of incitement, holding that his “No Reforms, No Election” campaign and calls to disrupt the October 2025 vote crossed the legal line, and sentenced him to four years in prison. Lissu, arrested on 9 April 2025 after a rally in Mbinga and charged the next day over social-media posts urging an election boycott, has now spent 577 days in detention; he called the treason case a political fabrication and has leave to appeal, and his party says it will appeal and seek bail in the meantime. Amnesty International condemned the outcome as the product of a deeply flawed process and demanded his release, and the Pan-African Solidarity Network called for an independent international investigation into alleged abuses around the 2025 election. The four-year term means Tanzania’s main opposition figure remains behind bars despite beating the charge that could have hanged him.

Latest · Global Affairs · Defence — Ratified 279–119 in Ankara7 November 2026

An Attack on One Is an Attack on All: Türkiye Ratifies the Mecca Defence Pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan

A new mutual-defence architecture in the Muslim world cleared a decisive hurdle on Friday. Türkiye’s parliament approved the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan by 279 votes to 119, completing Ankara’s ratification of a pact under which an external armed attack on any one of the three countries is treated as an attack on all. The vote binds together NATO’s second-largest military, the Gulf’s biggest economy and a nuclear-armed South Asian state at a moment when the nine-month Middle East war, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the US naval blockade of Iran have pushed every regional capital to hedge its security bets. For Pakistan, the pact formalises a defence relationship with Riyadh that has deepened through the war; for Saudi Arabia, it adds two substantial militaries to its deterrent posture beyond its traditional reliance on Washington; for Türkiye, it extends its strategic footprint from the Black Sea to the Arabian Sea. What the pact will mean in practice — joint planning, basing, procurement or only a political guarantee — now depends on the three governments’ implementing steps, but the parliamentary arithmetic in Ankara leaves no doubt about the intent: the agreement is meant to be read in Tehran, and in every other capital watching the Hormuz crisis.
