Serbia Wakes to a New Era: Student-Backed List Takes 139 Seats as Vučić Concedes After 12 Years

Serbia began Monday under a political order few thought possible two years ago. With almost 99% of votes counted, the State Election Commission put the student-backed list on 52.7% of the vote, worth 139 of the 250 seats in parliament, against 39.2% and 97 seats for the alliance led by Aleksandar Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party. Vučić, who has dominated the country as prime minister or president since 2014, conceded late on Sunday on his own party's sample count, and tens of thousands of people poured into the streets of Belgrade and other towns to celebrate, lighting flares and fireworks. The movement was born after 16 people died when a railway station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in 2024, a disaster protesters blamed on corruption, and it fielded 250 largely unknown candidates — academics, lawyers, doctors, actors, farmers and musicians. The harder question now is governing: the list has no single figurehead, no governing experience and no settled prime minister, and analysts warn that bureaucratic resistance, the economy, Kosovo and stalled EU talks will test whether a leaderless protest movement can hold together in power. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the winners and offered support for rule-of-law and anti-corruption reforms, while the Kremlin praised the defeated Vučić and said it hoped the new leaders would keep cooperation with Moscow.

“Decision Time” on Iran: Trump Weighs a Deal Against “Finish It Off” as a Third Carrier Group Heads to the Gulf

President Donald Trump has put his Iran choice in stark terms. In a Fox News interview broadcast on Sunday he said it was “getting to be decision time” and framed the options as two only: “Do I finish it off, which would be very easy to do, or do I make a deal? That's all.” He added: “We don't play games.” Trump has suggested any renewed strikes would come after the 3 November midterm elections, and Washington has sent additional forces toward the Persian Gulf, including a third aircraft carrier group, while reducing diplomatic and military staffing in parts of the Middle East as threats from Iran and the Houthis grow. He told reporters on Sunday there would be no strike that day. Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday, according to Reuters reporting cited in market coverage, and the last confirmed US strikes on Iran were on 1 September. In the interview Trump also named Pickaxe Mountain, a deeply buried site linked to Iran's nuclear programme, as a potential target, claiming the other three nuclear sites were “obliterated.” Officials quoted in the coverage warn the window between the midterms and the new Congress taking office could be especially dangerous, with a negotiated deal seen as the most likely route to ending the conflict.

Two Storms in 24 Hours: Boelo and Austen Kill One Man in Scotland and Cut Power to More Than 150,000 Homes

Northern Europe spent Monday clearing up after its first big autumn storms of the year arrived back to back. Storms Boelo and Austen passed through within 24 hours of each other, leaving more than 82,000 homes without electricity in Britain, nearly 60,000 in France and at least 10,000 in Germany — over 150,000 in total — and Britain's Energy Networks Association said more than 400,000 homes had lost power at some point. A 60-year-old man died when a tree fell on him in southwestern Scotland, two women were injured by a falling tree in central Scotland, and winds reached 135 km/h in Argyll and Bute, bringing down trees and power lines across roads and rail lines in Scotland and northern England. In France, grid operator Enedis said 288,000 homes were without power at Austen's peak on Saturday, with about 59,000 still cut off at midday Sunday, mainly in Normandy and Brittany, and 2,600 technicians working on repairs; a marathon runner in Lille was lightly hurt by a falling tree. In Germany the blackout around Freiburg forced the main hospital to limit itself to emergency cases and knocked out the mobile phone network, two people were seriously hurt by scaffolding blown down at a market in Nastatten, and hundreds of flights were cancelled or diverted across the region, including at Amsterdam's Schiphol. The UK Met Office warned Boelo posed a danger to life from flying debris and large waves.

No Return to Pre-War Energy Markets in 2027, IEA Chief Warns — and Nuclear Is “Coming Back” With 90 GW Under Construction

The head of the International Energy Agency has told governments and companies to stop planning for a quick return to normal. Speaking at the opening of Singapore International Energy Week on Monday, Executive Director Fatih Birol said he saw no realistic path for energy markets to get back in 2027 to the levels seen before the war with Iran began in late February, with oil approaching $120 a barrel and refined-product prices under extra pressure as winter diesel demand rises and refineries go into maintenance. Europe, he warned, faces a difficult winter: it has cut Russian gas imports while Qatar and the UAE cannot export gas at previous levels because of the war, leaving gas markets under significant strain. The longer consequence, in Birol's telling, is structural. The crisis has pushed countries to diversify away from vulnerable supply routes, electricity demand keeps climbing — driven by electric vehicles, air conditioning and other power-hungry technology — and nuclear power is central to the response: an IEA survey counts about 90 gigawatts of nuclear capacity now under construction worldwide, the highest level in four decades, spanning both traditional reactors and newer designs. “This energy crisis will even accelerate our entering the age of electricity,” he said.

Nurse Killed, Colleague Badly Hurt in Attack on Ebola Responders in Congo's Deadliest-Ever Outbreak

A nurse was killed and another health worker severely injured on Saturday when they were attacked while responding to an Ebola alert in Bafwasende, in the eastern Congolese province of Tshopo, the World Health Organization said. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, announcing the attack on Sunday, said everyone working to protect communities from the disease must be able to work “in dignity and safety” and that health care must always be protected. The WHO's Africa director, Mohamed Yakub Janabi, blamed misinformation and fear for fuelling violence against responders, writing: “Health is NOT a target.” Local authorities had not commented on the attack or on any suspects. The assault is the latest in a series that officials say is hampering the response to what health-ministry figures describe as the deadliest Ebola outbreak in Congo's history, with more than 9,800 cases and more than 4,700 deaths recorded since May, and responders say distrust of government and scepticism about the disease itself are complicating work in remote hot spots already short of staff and supplies. Last month a Congolese politician was beaten to death in North Kivu after publicly defending the official response on a radio show. Neighbouring Uganda moved on Sunday to tighten screening and impose a 21-day quarantine on citizens returning from Congo.

Third Night in the Rubble in Eastleigh: Fourth Survivor Pulled Out in Nairobi, but Red Cross Missing List Climbs to 47

Rescuers in Nairobi pulled two more people alive from a collapsed building on Sunday, bringing the number rescued to four as the search entered a third night and families said they were losing hope. The building — described as 18 storeys and still under construction — came down on Friday on a busy commercial street in the Eastleigh district, its lower floor giving way before the rest pancaked, trapping people in ground-floor and basement shops that officials say had been illegally occupied. Tailor Victor Onyango, rescued on Saturday, described seeing light come through the debris on Saturday morning, knowing it meant rescuers, and telling them a woman — shop owner Zaynaba Mohammed — was trapped behind the next section of rubble; she was pulled out after him, and both were treated in hospital and discharged. The numbers of the missing remain sharply disputed: the interior minister said on Friday that seven people were reported inside, while the Kenya Red Cross said on Sunday its missing-person reports had risen to 47. Of the 67 construction workers on site, 66 got out unharmed and one was injured. Nairobi County Governor Johnson Sakaja called for stricter enforcement against developers who flout building rules, calling current fines a “slap in the wrist” for those risking lives.

Meta's $95 Billion AI War Chest: $35 Billion Share Sale Now, $60 Billion More Later, on Capex of Up to $145 Billion This Year

Meta has joined the ranks of tech giants funding the AI build-out by selling shares on an enormous scale. In filings and announcements on Monday, the Facebook parent launched public offerings of about $35 billion — roughly $22.5 billion of Class A common stock and $12.5 billion of depositary shares representing mandatory convertible preferred stock — and said it plans a further at-the-market programme of up to $60 billion, potentially starting in the first quarter of 2027, taking the potential total to $95 billion for AI infrastructure and what it calls personal superintelligence. The move mirrors Alphabet, which recently raised $85 billion in equity for its own AI data-centre expansion, and underlines how the AI race is now being financed as much in capital markets as in server halls.

Judges Push Back on Trump's California Ballot Lawsuit: “Entirely Inconsistent” With the Elections Clause, a Trump Appointee Says

A federal appeals court gave the Trump administration a hard hearing on Sunday over its attempt to force California to set aside thousands of mail-in ballots ahead of the 3 November midterms. In a rare weekend sitting, two of the three judges on a 9th Circuit panel sharply questioned a Justice Department lawyer about the suit, filed on 15 October, which seeks an order requiring officials to separate ballots from people on a federal list of 20,000 said to be likely noncitizens registered to vote, and to withhold them from the count until citizenship is verified. Circuit Judge Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, told the department's lawyer that the requested injunction seemed “entirely inconsistent with the law that the California legislature has adopted pursuant to the Elections Clause” — the constitutional provision giving states authority over election procedures — and questioned why the government had appealed before the district court had even ruled. California argues its safeguards against noncitizen voting are robust and the federal list is unreliable: a Los Angeles County filing on a 200-name sample found cancelled or inactive registrations, people with no voting history and voters who appeared US-born. Bennett said the panel would rule as quickly as possible; Reuters noted all available evidence indicates voting by ineligible people, including noncitizens, is exceedingly rare.

Audi's First US Factory: $4.2 Billion, 4,500 Jobs and 200,000 SUVs a Year in Georgia — Announced at a Trump Rally

Audi will build its first factory in the United States, a $4.2 billion plant near Macon, Georgia, creating about 4,500 jobs and, at full capacity, producing 200,000 vehicles a year. President Donald Trump announced the deal at a campaign rally in Perry, Georgia, on Saturday, ten days before the midterm elections, with Audi chief executive Gernot Döllner alongside him; Audi confirmed the investment in its own release, saying construction is scheduled to begin next year with production in the early 2030s, and that North America would become a third major pillar of its production network alongside Europe and China. Döllner, facing criticism from Volkswagen's German unions over the rally appearance, told Reuters the decision followed months of planning and negotiations: “If the American president invites you, you accept the invitation.” The context is pressure: parent Volkswagen has been hit by US tariffs — which cost Audi about $1.4 billion in 2025 alone — along with Chinese competition and weak European demand, and in September the ten-brand group agreed a restructuring that could cut up to 100,000 jobs by 2030, the largest in global automotive history. Trump claimed the announcement as evidence of a “roaring comeback” in American auto manufacturing under his trade policy.

One Last Voyage for the Fastest Liner Ever: SS United States to Be Sunk off Florida as the World's Largest Artificial Reef

The ocean liner that still holds the record for the fastest transatlantic crossing by a passenger ship is to end its days on the seabed. Okaloosa County officials expect the SS United States to be sunk about 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin, on Florida's Gulf Coast, during the second week of November, weather permitting, to become what they intend to be the world's largest artificial reef. The nearly 1,000-foot (305-metre) vessel shattered the transatlantic speed record on its maiden voyage in 1952 and carried presidents and Hollywood stars before retiring in 1969. Preservation groups tried for decades to save her but were defeated by the cost; the county bought the ship for $1 million in 2024, remediation work is complete and final approvals are in place. Once down, she will join more than 500 artificial reefs in the region, and supporters hope a barnacle-encrusted wreck will draw divers, anglers and tourists, generating millions of dollars a year for dive shops, charter boats and hotels. Salvaged pieces — including her funnels and radar mast — will be displayed in a land-based museum, so something of the ship stays above the waterline.
