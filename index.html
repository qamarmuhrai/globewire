Latest · Global Affairs · Antalya — Waiting on Draft Three21 November 2026

COP31 Enters Saturday With No Deal, and Anger Rising Over a Text That Will Not Name Fossil Fuels

The climate summit was meant to close on Friday evening. Instead, nearly 200 delegations in Antalya woke on Saturday still waiting for a third draft of the deal that will define COP31, after overnight consultations failed to bridge the summit’s central divide. The second draft, cut from 144 paragraphs to 80, contains no mention of coal, oil or gas at all: language in Monday’s first text urging a faster move away from fossil fuels was removed after objections from Saudi Arabia and other big producers, and a proposed $120 billion-a-year adaptation finance target for developing countries by 2035 was replaced with a vaguer promise of further talks on tripling such finance. What survives is host Türkiye’s electrification goal — raising electricity to 35% of global final energy use by 2035 — though the text does not require that power to be renewable, and a first-ever acknowledgement in a COP decision that warming is set to overshoot the Paris 1.5°C limit. A group of countries led by France and Panama pressed the presidency on Friday for a far stronger outcome, listing six priorities including an explicit fossil-fuel shift and faster methane cuts, while the EU warned the draft risks making this a “fossil summit” and civil-society groups rejected it outright. President Murat Kurum promised the new text on Saturday morning and vowed delegates would not leave until consensus is found; summit-watchers surveyed by Carbon Brief put the likely finish late on Sunday. The United States stayed away for a second straight year.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · The Hague — A Verdict Nine Years On20 November 2026

Myanmar Calls the World Court “Unfair” After a Historic First: a State Found Guilty of Genocide

Myanmar’s military-backed government on Friday dismissed as “unfair” the International Court of Justice ruling that it committed genocide against the Rohingya — a judgment without precedent in the court’s 80-year history. By nine votes to six, the judges found on Thursday that Myanmar’s 2017 clearance operations in northern Rakhine State, which killed at least 10,000 Rohingya and drove more than 730,000 people into Bangladesh, were carried out with intent to destroy the group in part, citing indiscriminate killings, widespread sexual violence and extreme violence against children. It is the first time the World Court has held a state itself responsible for genocide, in a case brought in 2019 by The Gambia, and only the fourth such finding by any international court since the Holocaust. The court ordered Myanmar to stop all genocidal acts, investigate and prosecute those responsible, and make reparations: facilitating the safe, voluntary return of the Rohingya, restoring or replacing lost property, searching for the disappeared and rehabilitating survivors, with a report on restitution due within two years. A government spokesperson stressed that the decision was not unanimous. The ICJ cannot enforce its rulings, but rights groups say the verdict strengthens parallel efforts to prosecute individuals and will echo in other cases, including the one concerning Gaza. For survivors in the camps of Cox’s Bazar, advocates called it a turning point — though return home remains distant.

Latest · Europe · Warsaw — Missiles on NATO Soil20 November 2026

Two Russian Missiles and a Drone Came Down in Poland. Warsaw Calls It a Deliberate Provocation

The war crossed NATO’s border in hardware on Friday. During Russia’s massive overnight assault on Ukraine — more than 100 missiles and some 400 drones that killed at least nine people, damaged three of Kyiv’s Dnipro bridges and knocked out power across the country — three Russian objects violated Polish airspace and crashed on Polish territory. Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz confirmed a Kh-101 cruise missile near Nowy Lipowiec, about 60 kilometres from the Ukrainian border, a second missile near Rudnik nad Sanem, and a jet-powered drone near Mołodycz; nobody was injured. Prime Minister Donald Tusk, who convened an emergency security meeting, said it appeared “we are dealing with an intentional provocation,” noting the timing: thousands of troops from 11 countries of the Coalition of the Willing are exercising in southeastern Poland, some 75 kilometres from one crash site, rehearsing a possible postwar mission in Ukraine. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte called the incursions a “grave violation” of Polish airspace, said alliance jets had scrambled, and is due in Warsaw on Saturday; Poland says it will ask NATO for more support on the eastern flank. President Trump had earlier described Russian weapons landing in Poland as possibly “by mistake” — an explanation Warsaw pointedly declined to adopt while investigations continue.

Latest · Europe · Copenhagen — The Rapper and the Gang20 November 2026

Denmark Expels Russia’s Defence Attaché Over an Alleged Plot to Kill a Rapper at His Own Concert

Denmark has given Russia’s defence attaché seven days to leave the country after its security service, PET, concluded that Russian intelligence planned to assassinate the Ukrainian-American rapper Kyivstoner on Danish soil. The plot was stopped on 25 September, when police intercepted three men in a car near the venue north of Copenhagen where the 34-year-old performer — born Albert Vasiliev, holder of both Ukrainian and US citizenship — was about to play; firearms and knives were found in the vehicle. Investigators suspect the banned street gang Loyal to Familia was recruited to carry out the killing. Two men were placed under formal investigation on Friday for attempted murder and for assisting a foreign intelligence service. “It is our clear assessment that the assassination attempt was planned by the Russian intelligence services,” PET chief Rasmus Kieffer-Kristensen said, calling it the first Russian assassination attempt seen in Denmark and evidence of a more aggressive, risk-taking Moscow. Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen summoned the Russian ambassador, said Copenhagen did not want to sever diplomatic relations but intended a “very, very strong signal,” and raised possible restrictions on Schengen visas for Russians. The Russian embassy called the allegations baseless and absurd, and spokeswoman Maria Zakharova promised a response.

Latest · Europe · Tallinn — The Border Comes Down20 November 2026

Estonia Will Shut Its Russian Border for Six Months. Moscow Calls It a New Iron Curtain

Estonia’s government confirmed on Friday that all its road border crossings with Russia — Narva, Koidula, Luhamaa and the smaller posts — will close from 1 December for at least six months, after which Tallinn will decide whether to extend the shutdown. The decision follows what Estonian officials describe as the second Russian-ordered sabotage incident this year: an attempted arson attack on drone maker Threod Systems, which the Internal Security Service attributes to Russia’s GRU military intelligence and which led to eight arrests. Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna said closing the crossings would reduce Russia’s ability to conduct hostile activity and curb sanctions circumvention, framing the move as a consequence Moscow brought on itself. In Moscow, Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova accused Estonia of erecting “a new Iron Curtain under the false pretence of some sort of threat from the East” and warned Russia would take political and diplomatic measures in response; the Kremlin has denied ordering the arson attempt and calls such accusations a pattern of false allegations. Estonia follows Finland, which shut its Russian border in 2023 and has kept it closed, while Latvia and Lithuania still maintain open crossings — leaving the Baltic frontier with Russia increasingly sealed.

Latest · Europe · Milan to London — “Fight for Our Future”20 November 2026

Young Protesters Marched in 25 Countries on One Day, Against Rents, Rearmament and the Price of Everything

Friday’s protests were planned country by country but felt like one movement. A youth-led coalition of more than 50 groups coordinated demonstrations across more than 25 European countries, linking grievances that rhyme from city to city: soaring housing costs, deteriorating schools and universities, stagnant pay, the climate crisis — and, cutting across all of them, anger that governments are finding money for rearmament while public services shrink. Italy saw the largest turnouts, as the CGIL union’s general strike filled more than 100 squares; tens of thousands marched in Milan, where 18-year-old Olivia Ferrario told AFP, “If the government does not listen to us, we cannot remain silent,” while in Rome some protesters threw bottles and smoke bombs at police and demonstrators burned a cardboard tank. Organisers said more than 40,000 took part across Switzerland, where a few clashes after the main rally brought tear gas and water cannon. In Berlin roughly 4,000 gathered at Alexanderplatz, many fearing Germany’s rapid rearmament could bring back conscription; in London, school and university students marched on Parliament with placards reading “Fight for our futures, not their wars.” Madrid demanded education funding, Vienna rallied outside its university, and France — whose student protests since September inspired much of the wave — is set to continue into the weekend around the anniversary of the Yellow Vests.

Latest · Americas · New York — 235 Months20 November 2026

An “Attack on US Sovereignty”: 19½ Years for the Man Who Tried to Hire a Hitman to Kill a Sikh Activist

A federal judge in Manhattan on Friday sentenced Nikhil Gupta, 55, to 235 months in prison — more than nineteen and a half years — for his part in a foiled plot to assassinate Sikh separatist leader Gurpatwant Singh Pannun in New York City. Gupta pleaded guilty in February to murder-for-hire, conspiracy to commit murder-for-hire and conspiracy to commit money laundering, admitting he worked with Indian government official Vikash Yadav, who recruited him in 2023 to arrange the killing for $100,000; a $15,000 advance was delivered to a supposed hitman who was in fact an undercover US officer. Prosecutors had sought about 24 years, the defence 12; Judge Victor Marrero imposed the bottom of the guidelines range and described the conspiracy as an attack on US sovereignty. Gupta, arrested in Prague in 2023 and extradited after nearly a year of proceedings, apologised in court and faces deportation to India after his sentence. The case has strained Washington–New Delhi ties — India denies involvement — and has long been linked in investigators’ minds to the 2023 killing of another Sikh separatist, Hardeep Singh Nijjar, outside a temple in British Columbia. Pannun, a US-Canadian citizen and lawyer for Sikhs for Justice, campaigns for a sovereign Sikh state in Punjab.

Latest · Americas · Havana / Washington — Home After Nine Years20 November 2026

Quietly Flown Home: the American Teacher Cuba Held for Nearly a Decade Is Free, With No Exchange

It happened in September, and almost nobody was told. Alina López Miyares, a 68-year-old Cuban-American special-education teacher detained in Cuba since 2017, was released and flown home by a US government team that travelled quietly to Havana, the State Department confirmed on Friday after CNN reported the release. “We welcomed Alina Lopez’s return to the United States in September. There was no exchange for her release,” a spokesperson said, declining further detail out of respect for her privacy. López was born in Cuba, left as a child and became a US citizen; advocates say she was lured back in 2017 by her estranged husband, whom they allege was a Cuban intelligence operative, then arrested, tried by a military court and sentenced to 13 years for espionage. She was freed from prison in 2022 under international pressure but barred from leaving the island, then imprisoned again in 2023. A UN working group had ruled her detention arbitrary and demanded her release, and the James Foley Foundation, which campaigns for Americans wrongfully detained abroad, says she has urgent and worsening medical needs. The release comes amid a renewed US pressure campaign on Havana — sanctions and an oil blockade layered on an embargo dating to 1962 — making an unreciprocated humanitarian release all the more notable.

Latest · Middle East · Jurf al-Sakhar — The Compromise Entry20 November 2026

Iraqi Forces Entered the Militia Stronghold That Was Closed to the State for 12 Years — by Negotiation, Not Battle

For more than a decade, Jurf al-Sakhar, some 60 kilometres southwest of Baghdad, answered to Kataib Hezbollah, the Iran-aligned armed group that helped retake the town from Islamic State and then kept it as a closed, militarised zone. On Friday, Iraqi government forces drove in under the national flag and took up positions in the centre — not after a battle, but after a compromise that averted one. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi had ordered the group out this week, accusing it of running illicit refineries that siphoned state oil worth hundreds of millions of dollars; Kataib Hezbollah denied the charge and had warned that anyone entering would do so “over our dead bodies.” Under Thursday’s deal, reportedly brokered with the help of Iran’s Quds Force commander Esmail Qaani, the group withdraws within a month while a joint force — an army brigade, Federal Police, Energy Police and two Popular Mobilisation Forces brigades — secures the pipelines under a new control headquarters that the Federal Police will run. An AFP correspondent watched police pass through a Kataib Hezbollah checkpoint where the group’s fighters still stood. The deployment, driven in part by US and Gulf pressure to rein in pro-Iran factions, underlines the limits of Baghdad’s authority: al-Zaidi has already pushed the deadline for bringing all militia weapons under state control back to at least June 2027.

Bangladesh Burns Again: Arson, Bombs and 124 Arrests as Hasina Vows to Come Home to a Death Sentence

Fresh arson attacks erupted across Bangladesh on Friday as authorities restored communications with Gopalganj, the hometown of convicted former prime minister Sheikh Hasina, after days of unrest over her promised return. Hasina, 79, living in exile in India, has vowed to come back next month despite being sentenced to death in absentia for crimes against humanity over the deadly 2024 crackdown that ended her rule; her banned Awami League called strikes this week marking the anniversary of the sentence. The latest wave followed six days, from 14 to 19 November, in which at least 21 districts saw crude-bomb explosions, arson, vandalism and road blockades that killed four people: in Gopalganj, protesters wielding machetes torched the Tungipara police station after storming it to free detainees, and the homes of three MPs were attacked; police data record 70 crude-bomb explosions and 57 vehicle arsons between 7 and 17 November alone. On Friday a petrol bomb was thrown at the Sylhet residence of junior foreign minister Humaiun Kobir, the Chittagong family home of Nobel laureate and former interim leader Muhammad Yunus was targeted, and Grameen Bank says more than 20 of its branches have been attacked this month, with an internal audit counting 72 incidents. Police said 124 people linked to the Awami League were arrested in Dhaka in 24 hours, and hundreds of paramilitary troops remain deployed; three people have been killed in the current unrest.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · Shenzhen — Joint Development, Carefully Worded20 November 2026

Prabowo and Xi Talk South China Sea “Joint Development” — and Sign $15 Billion of Deals Around the Subject

Indonesia’s President Prabowo Subianto met China’s President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen on Friday, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, and the most sensitive item on the table was the South China Sea. Jakarta said both sides saw prospects for partnership in the strategic waterway, including progress in talks on areas for joint development; Prabowo told Xi, “Our relationship, we think, keeps growing stronger. We have reached a more aligned understanding.” China’s Xinhua report pointedly did not mention the sea by name, referring only to positive progress in joint maritime development — a gap that reflects how contested the wording is. A November 2024 maritime deal, in which the two agreed to “joint development in areas of overlapping claims,” drew criticism that Jakarta risked legitimising Beijing’s vast claims; Indonesia’s foreign ministry insisted at the time that it does not recognise those claims, and Prabowo has pledged to safeguard sovereignty. The economics around the diplomacy are large: the meeting followed a Wednesday agreement launching four strategic partnerships worth $71.8 billion, including 10 smart cities, and a Friday business forum added 35 deals worth $15 billion spanning data centres, renewables, vaccines and manufacturing. The two sides also launched negotiations on a comprehensive economic partnership, Prabowo confirmed Indonesia’s ratification of membership in China’s World AI Cooperation Organisation, and Xi urged progress on the $7.3 billion China-funded bullet train alongside deeper energy, minerals and clean-energy cooperation.

Latest · Africa · Eastern Congo — The Virus That Hides20 November 2026

11,000 Cases In, Scientists Are Learning Why This Ebola Is So Hard to See — and Why Children Are Dying Fastest

Six months into the Democratic Republic of Congo’s devastating Ebola outbreak, researchers are finally piecing together how the rare Bundibugyo strain behaves — knowledge that may explain why it has spread at double the pace of the 2014–16 West African epidemic, the largest on record. The outbreak, declared in May but probably transmitting months earlier, has passed 11,000 cases and 5,500 deaths, and there is still no licensed vaccine or treatment for this species of the virus. One finding is changing how medics screen for it: the bleeding long treated as Ebola’s signature appears in only about 5 to 10 percent of Bundibugyo cases, Africa CDC’s Yap Boum told Reuters, and often arrives late — so patients who look like malaria or a stomach illness stay home, where roughly 70 percent of the sick are still dying in their communities. Children are hit hardest of all: 1,207 confirmed cases in under-fives, of whom 844 have died, a toll of about seven in ten, with gastrointestinal symptoms more common than fever and early diagnosis therefore harder. Experts caution that eastern Congo’s humanitarian crisis, poor roads and scarce, messy data make firm conclusions risky. “Soon we’ll be able to make some bold statements about what’s the same, what’s different, and what we need to adjust,” said the WHO’s incident manager, Catherine Smallwood.
