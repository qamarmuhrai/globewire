Latest · Asia-Pacific · Cyclone Arnab28 October 2026

Cyclone Arnab Begins Landfall Near Yanam: Winds Gusting to 150 km/h, Trees Uprooted and Power Cut — No Deaths Reported So Far

Very severe cyclonic storm Arnab began making landfall on India's Andhra Pradesh coast in the early hours of Wednesday, near Yanam close to Kakinada, bringing high-speed winds and heavy rain that uprooted trees and disrupted power supply, authorities said. The India Meteorological Department put winds over the landfall area at 125–135 km/h, gusting to 150 km/h, over the following four hours before easing gradually, and warned of a storm surge of 1.5 to 2.5 metres above normal tide that could inundate low-lying areas over the next six hours; the landfall process was expected to be complete by about 4 a.m. No loss of life had been reported so far, the Andhra Pradesh chief minister's office said, although trees were uprooted in several places. The storm arrived after days of preparation: thousands of people were evacuated along the east coast — more than 84,000 in Andhra Pradesh alone by one count, with nearly 89,000 along the Kakinada coast moved towards 101 relief camps and Odisha planning to move 48,000 more — and the state identified 2,199 cyclone shelters and shifted 2,360 pregnant women to medical facilities, with 12 NDRF and 10 SDRF teams deployed and more than 8,400 personnel mobilised for power restoration. More than 90 trains were cancelled, diverted or short-terminated and flights at Visakhapatnam were cancelled or diverted. One death was reported on Tuesday, when a 65-year-old man touched snapped live wires in Mangalapalem. Arnab swept in from the Bay of Bengal, the basin that produced the 1999 Odisha super cyclone that killed nearly 10,000 people; rain and strong winds are expected to continue after the crossing, with heavy rain also forecast inland for parts of Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal and Jharkhand, and Nepal on alert for the storm's remnants from 29 to 31 October.

Latest · Middle East · Israel Election27 October 2026

Israel Exit Polls: No Winner — Netanyahu's Bloc and Eisenkot's Bloc Both Stuck on About 50–53 Seats, Arab Parties Surge to a Record 17–18

Israel's first election since the 7 October 2023 attacks ended in deadlock on Tuesday night, with exit polls from Channels 11, 12 and 13 all showing Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc and the opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot falling short of the 61 seats needed to govern the 120-member Knesset. Netanyahu's bloc was projected at 50 seats in all three polls; Eisenkot's bloc — Yashar, Together (B'Yachad), Yisrael Beytenu and the Democrats — at 52 to 53. Netanyahu's Likud remained the largest single party on 24–25 seats, ahead of Eisenkot's Yashar on 21–23. The pivotal result was the Arab parties: the Joint List and Ra'am were projected to win a combined 17–18 seats, a record, which means the combined anti-Netanyahu vote reaches 70 — but Eisenkot had ruled out forming a government with the Arab parties before the vote, leaving neither side with a clear path to a coalition. Final turnout was 77.7% when polls closed at 10 p.m., up 7.1 points on 2022 and the highest pace recorded since at least 1973. The opposition declared victory on the projections; Yair Lapid said Netanyahu's bloc had suffered a "crushing defeat" and that "the public has told them to get out," while Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar called the celebration premature and noted Likud was still the biggest party. Netanyahu himself had not responded as counting began; the first official results were expected around 1 a.m., with the full count possibly stretching to Friday because of the volume of absentee ballots. An inconclusive result leaves Netanyahu in office in a caretaker capacity while weeks of coalition bargaining begin.

Latest · Africa · DR Congo Ebola27 October 2026

Ebola in Congo Passes 10,000 Confirmed Cases and 4,887 Deaths — the Fastest-Growing and Deadliest Outbreak in the Country's History

Confirmed Ebola cases in the Democratic Republic of the Congo have passed 10,000, government data released on Tuesday showed — 10,060 cases and 4,887 deaths — making this the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record anywhere and the deadliest in Congo's history. Nearly half of those confirmed infected have died. The wider outbreak is now the second-largest and second-deadliest ever recorded, behind only the 2014–2016 West African epidemic, which infected 28,600 people and killed more than 11,000 over two years; the Congo outbreak has accumulated cases and deaths at least three times faster in the months since it was declared on 15 May. It is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, a rare Ebola strain for which no vaccine or treatment has been approved, although the World Health Organization announced on Friday that the first clinical trial of a vaccine — the Tumaini vaccine, already being administered in Ituri — has begun. Containment is failing on the key measure: about 80% of new cases are arising outside the transmission chains health authorities are tracking, while the Africa CDC says at least 95% of cases must come from known contacts for an outbreak to be considered under control. Response teams are also battling armed conflict, poor roads and violence fuelled by mistrust and misinformation. The virus has spread farther across Africa than any previous Ebola outbreak, recorded in five countries: Kenya has reported nine cases after a man who travelled from Congo via Uganda died in Nairobi, Nigeria reported its first case last week — the wife of a Swiss diplomat, flown with her husband to hospitals in Switzerland on Monday — and Uganda, which recorded 20 mostly imported cases in May and June, was declared Ebola-free in August but now requires citizens returning from Congo to quarantine for 21 days; France has also recorded cases, with none currently active there or in Uganda.

Latest · Middle East · Jordan & Syria27 October 2026

Jordan Says It Killed More Than 20 Islamic State Fighters in Strikes on Syria — Syria Counts Seven Dead, Including Three Children

Jordan said on Tuesday that its air strikes on southern Syria the previous evening killed more than 20 Islamic State militants, in retaliation for Friday's gun attack on a funeral service at the Greek Orthodox St George's Church in Hammoud, in Jordan's Karak governorate, which killed nine people and wounded dozens. Government spokesman Mohammad al-Momani said Jordan regretted civilian casualties from the operation. Syria's account differs sharply: its Civil Defence service said at least four sites in rural Daraa and Sweida provinces were hit, and crews recovered seven bodies — five of them a man, a woman and three children from one family killed in a strike on a tent in the village of Al-Tai in Daraa, an 80-year-old man in Al Shayyah, and a 60-year-old man in a residential building in Shahba in Sweida; Daraa health officials later put the toll at eight, including an elderly man and three children, with four wounded. The two governments also dispute coordination: Jordan's armed forces said the operation was conducted in coordination with Damascus, while Syria's government said the strikes went ahead without prior coordination or notification and that it had opened an investigation; Syria's border authority closed the Nassib crossing — the only passenger and goods crossing between the two countries — suspending traffic in both directions. Hours before the strikes, Jordanian security forces had arrested three suspects — brothers Hazza and Meshal Zaidan Mohammed and their brother-in-law Ahmad Sami Hussein — after a dawn shootout at an abandoned house where they had barricaded themselves; all three were wounded and taken to hospital. Jordanian officials linked the church attackers to groups operating in southern Syria, though Islamic State has not claimed responsibility for the church attack, and Jordan's public security chief described the gunmen as extremists who acted independently of an established network.

Latest · Americas · Brazil & United States27 October 2026

Trump Calls Lula's Win 'Another Rigged Election' — but Bolsonaro's Own Party Recognises the Result, and Flávio Leaves for the US

US President Donald Trump has described President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's re-election victory in Brazil as looking like "another rigged election," criticising the Supreme Court's decision to extend Sunday's runoff voting by two hours and saying the way the vote was tabulated "looked so corrupt, so terrible" — while offering no evidence, saying he had not taken sides, and adding: "I like Lula, I get along with him very well." Lula won the runoff with 51.8% of the vote, defeating Flávio Bolsonaro by nearly 4.4 million votes to secure a fourth, non-consecutive term starting 5 January 2027, after overturning a first-round deficit — the first candidate since Brazil's return to direct elections in 1989 to win the presidency after finishing second in the first round. Flávio Bolsonaro refused to concede and said he would seek to have the result annulled over the extension, but on Monday his Liberal Party formally recognised the outcome in a statement signed by party head Valdemar Costa Neto — "We recognize the presidential election results" — while saying the issues raised needed clarification; an analysis of electoral records found fewer than 35,000 votes, about 0.03% of the total, were cast in the extra two hours. Both campaigns had in fact requested more voting time — Lula's over bus shortages and transport disruption, Bolsonaro's over heavy rain in the south. On Tuesday, an ally confirmed to Reuters that Flávio Bolsonaro had left Brazil for the United States, two days after the defeat; he had earlier said he would comply with a federal police order to hand over his passports after visiting his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro — who is serving a 27-year term over the 2022 coup attempt — and denied planning to flee. Trump, asked whether he would act on his criticism, said: "I'm not running for Brazil, I'm running for the USA."

Latest · Africa · Russia-Africa Summit28 October 2026

Third Russia-Africa Summit Opens in Moscow: 48 Countries, 22 Heads of State, and a Ghanaian President Arriving to Ask Putin for a Ukraine Ceasefire

African leaders are gathering in Moscow for the third Russia-Africa Summit on 28–29 October, co-chaired by President Vladimir Putin and Burundi's President Évariste Ndayishimiye, the current African Union chairperson. The Kremlin says 48 of Africa's 54 UN-recognised countries have confirmed participation, 22 of them at head-of-state level — a stronger presidential turnout than the 17 who attended the 2023 summit in St Petersburg, though below the 43 leaders at the first summit in Sochi in 2019. The agenda covers trade, investment, science and technology, energy, food security, digital transformation and artificial intelligence, and the meeting is expected to adopt a joint declaration, a Russia-Africa Partnership Action Plan for 2027–2029 and a declaration on cooperation in the civilian use of AI, alongside intergovernmental and commercial agreements; Moscow also hosted the eighth summit of the Gas Exporting Countries Forum on Tuesday. Russia's foreign ministry has accused Western governments of pressuring African capitals to stay away — a charge Ghana's President John Dramani Mahama answered by saying partnerships should deliver investment and fair returns rather than be dictated by competition among outside powers. Mahama, who arrived in Moscow on Monday, has said he will use the visit to press Putin for a ceasefire in Ukraine, framing the appeal through Ghana's non-aligned stance as "friends to all and enemies to none," and is expected to hold a bilateral meeting with Putin on the summit's margins. Zimbabwe's President Mnangagwa, the presidents of Mali, Mozambique and the Republic of Congo, Senegal's prime minister, Uganda's Vice President Jessica Alupo and Tanzania's Prime Minister Mwigulu Nchemba — representing President Samia Suluhu Hassan — are among those in Moscow or travelling there.

Latest · Europe · Ukraine War27 October 2026

A Local Ceasefire Takes Hold in Oleshky: Guns Fall Silent in One Occupied Town as November Trilateral Talks Are Set — Peskov Says They Do Not Limit Fighting Elsewhere

A localised humanitarian ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, across the Dnipro River from Kherson, is the centrepiece of a tentative diplomatic opening in the four-and-a-half-year war. The arrangement — brokered through US First Lady Melania Trump, who worked directly with Russian and Ukrainian leadership, the White House said — suspends all military action in the town so food, medicine and clean water can reach civilians and residents who want to leave can be evacuated through a designated safe passage; Russia's chief of the general staff, Valery Gerasimov, said Russian troops would halt fire from 4 a.m. local time on 27 October to let the humanitarian operation start, and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called Melania Trump's contribution "significant," while noting the evacuation could take several days. Oleshky, occupied since February 2022, has faced a deepening food crisis. Around the truce, Ukraine says negotiators from Ukraine, Russia and the United States have agreed to technical-level talks in the first half of November, in Abu Dhabi or possibly Switzerland, with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy hoping a leaders' meeting follows; he said the timing "will depend on the Russians." The limits are explicit: Peskov has said the upcoming trilateral talks do not restrict military action elsewhere, and the ceasefire covers one town, not the front — it follows the UN commission's finding, reported in the previous edition, that Russia's energy-infrastructure campaign amounts to crimes against humanity, and a recent rift between Washington and Kyiv, including friction over limits placed on Ukraine's use of the Starlink system, which President Trump said he would straighten out with SpaceX chief Elon Musk if needed.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · India & Pakistan27 October 2026

First Since 2023 — and First Since the May 2025 Conflict: India Invites a Pakistani Minister to Delhi for the SCO's First Civilisational Dialogue

India has invited Pakistan's Minister for National Heritage and Culture, Aurangzeb Khan Khichi, to the inaugural Civilisational Dialogue Forum of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO), to be held in New Delhi from 20 to 22 November — the first time India has hosted a Pakistani minister since the 2023 SCO foreign ministers' meeting in Goa, attended by then foreign minister Bilawal Bhutto, and the first such invitation since the four-day India-Pakistan conflict of May 2025 that followed the Pahalgam terror attack. The forum, proposed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the SCO leaders' summit in Tianjin last year and adopted by the SCO Council, will be organised by India's culture and external affairs ministries and inaugurated by Modi at Purana Quila, Delhi's 16th-century fort built by Emperor Humayun — a site whose excavations trace some 3,000 years of settlement from pre-Mauryan times through the Mughal era. Culture ministers from all 10 SCO member states, including China, Russia, Iran and Belarus, plus representatives of 15 dialogue partners and observer countries — more than 25 countries in all — have been invited, with the aim of strengthening people-to-people ties and showcasing shared heritage, art, literature and traditions. Diplomatic sources say Pakistan has not yet confirmed who will lead its delegation. The invitation is part of India's obligation as host to invite every SCO member; bilateral ties between Delhi and Islamabad have otherwise been frozen for a decade, since the 2016 Pathankot and Uri attacks, even as both countries continue to meet inside multilateral forums — India sent its SCO national coordinator to Islamabad in September ahead of Pakistan's turn hosting the SCO leaders' summit next year. Separately on Tuesday, the two-day BRICS Parliamentary Forum adopted a joint declaration condemning the 2025 Pahalgam attack and all terrorism, and calling for UN Security Council reform.

Rubio to Host the G20 Foreign Ministers in Atlanta on 30–31 October, as Washington Says It Is Returning the Grouping to Its 'Core Economic Mission'

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio will host the G20 Foreign Ministers' Meeting in Atlanta, Georgia, on 30–31 October, the State Department has announced — the first major ministerial gathering of the United States' 2026 G20 presidency, and the staging post for President Donald Trump's G20 Leaders' Summit, set for Trump National Doral in Miami on 14–15 December. Latest · Business & Technology · Microsoft26 October 2026

Microsoft Offers Voluntary Exit Packages to About 60,000 Staff — a Quarter of Its Workforce — as AI Spending Squeezes Costs

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees — roughly a quarter of its global workforce — as the company cuts costs while pouring money into artificial intelligence, it said on Monday. Staff on select teams in the United States, Canada, India and Britain, at vice-president level and below, are eligible — reporting puts the participating groups as including Xbox, Windows, Copilot and CoreAI in full, with parts of Cloud + AI also in — while some groups, including parts of the data-centre, sales, marketing and security organisations, are excluded. Chief people officer Amy Coleman told staff the Voluntary Exit Offer was about "creating greater flexibility and, where possible, more ways for employees to shape their next chapter"; the offer is time-limited, with terms reported to resemble standard severance, and is not based on age, service or performance. It is Microsoft's second such offer this year: in April it offered early retirement to about 7% of its US workforce, and around 30% of those eligible took it. Microsoft employed 223,000 people at the end of June, down from 228,000 at the end of 2025, and reports quarterly earnings later this week. The move lands amid a wider Big Tech squeeze driven by AI capital spending: Netflix is reportedly preparing to cut about 6% of its staff, around 1,000 jobs; eBay laid off 1,000 employees earlier this month after 800 earlier in the year; and Meta cut about 10% of its workforce, some 8,000 people, in May — even as industry figures such as Anthropic chief Dario Amodei warn the technology itself could drive mass job losses.
