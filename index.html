Latest · Europe · Kyiv / Poland — Missiles Across a NATO Border21 November 2026

Russia’s Biggest Barrage of the Month Kills Eight in Ukraine — and Lands Missiles in Poland

Russia unleashed its largest strike package of November on Ukraine overnight, and this time the war physically crossed into NATO territory. Ukrainian officials counted 27 ballistic missiles, 84 cruise missiles and more than 400 strike drones fired in waves timed 20 to 30 minutes apart, a pattern President Volodymyr Zelensky said was designed to complicate rescue work. Eight people were killed — six of them in Kyiv and two in Lviv — and about 20 more were wounded in a later strike on Kryvyi Rih. In the capital, three bridges across the Dnipro River — the Podilsko-Voskresensky, Darnytskyi and Southern crossings — were damaged along with energy lines, warehouses and homes across at least 15 regions. Air defences intercepted roughly 94% of the cruise missiles and drones but only about one ballistic missile in nine. In Poland, a Kh-101 cruise missile came down near the village of Nowy Lipowiec, a second suspected missile fell near Rudnik nad Sanem and a jet-powered drone near Mołodycz — no casualties, but Prime Minister Donald Tusk called the incursions a deliberate provocation, possibly timed to coincide with multinational military exercises under way nearby. NATO scrambled Polish, Swedish and Dutch jets in response, France has called for a UN Security Council meeting, and Moscow confirmed it had launched a “massive” attack, hours after President Vladimir Putin had vowed a “painful” response to Ukrainian strikes on the Crimea bridge.

Latest · Middle East · Strait of Hormuz — Tankers Hit, Drone Claim21 November 2026

Two Tankers Struck Leaving Hormuz, a Third Catches Fire on a Mine, and Iran Claims a US Drone

The world’s most sensitive oil chokepoint had a dangerous Saturday. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported two tankers hit by “unknown projectiles” as they left the Strait of Hormuz: one struck on the stern about 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, and a second hit in its engine room 17 nautical miles east of the port. No crew were reported injured in either incident, and any environmental damage remained unconfirmed. Separately, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said a tanker that entered a restricted area caught fire after striking naval mines south of the strait, near Oman’s Al Ghanam Island, and warned shipping away from designated zones. The Guards also claimed their aerospace force shot down a US MQ-9 Reaper drone west of the strait — by their count the 58th such aircraft destroyed — saying it had been providing aerial cover for transiting vessels; Washington offered no immediate confirmation, though a US official had acknowledged American jets struck an Iranian missile site on Qeshm Island in the strait on Tuesday. The incidents stack new risk onto a waterway already at the heart of the US–Iran conflict, through which a large share of the world’s seaborne oil passes.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · The Hague — A First in 80 Years19 November 2026

The World Court Finds Myanmar Guilty of Genocide Against the Rohingya — but Return Home Remains Distant

For the first time in its 80-year history, the International Court of Justice has found that a state committed genocide. In a 9–6 decision read out over three hours in The Hague on Thursday, the court ruled that Myanmar’s security forces committed genocide against the Rohingya of northern Rakhine State during the 2017 crackdown that drove more than 730,000 people into Bangladesh. Court President Yuji Iwasawa said the evidence showed at least 10,000 Rohingya killed, alongside widespread sexual violence and extreme violence against children, and that the “only reasonable inference” from the pattern — villages systematically destroyed and repopulated by others — was an intent to destroy the group. The judges also found Myanmar had failed in its duty to prevent and punish the crime. The orders that follow are sweeping: halt all violence, investigate and prosecute those responsible, and make reparations, including facilitating the safe, voluntary and dignified return of the displaced, restoring or replacing lost property, accounting for the disappeared and ensuring proper burials, with a report on restitution due to the court within two years. The case was brought by The Gambia in 2019 under the 1948 Genocide Convention. The verdict is final and cannot be appealed — but the ICJ has no enforcement arm, and whether Myanmar’s rulers comply, and whether refugee families in Cox’s Bazar can actually go home, remains the open question. Lawyers are also watching the reasoning closely for what it may signal in the separate case concerning Gaza.

Latest · Global Affairs · Antalya — Overtime, Day Two21 November 2026

COP31 Talks Into a Second Overtime Day as Crews Pack Up the Venue Around the Negotiators

The strangest image of this year’s climate summit may be its last: work crews boxing up electronics in the pavilions of the COP31 venue in Antalya on Saturday morning while, down emptied hallways, negotiators from nearly 200 countries tried for another day to agree on a final text that was due on Friday. The sticking points have not moved. A slimmed-down draft of the deal contains no mention of fossil fuels at all — language on limiting their use that appeared earlier in the week was stripped out — and offers a call to at least triple adaptation finance without binding new money, against developing-country needs estimated in the trillions by 2030. The talks follow the UN’s warning that the world is now likely to overshoot the Paris goal of holding warming to 1.5°C. “COP31 is in a bleak overtime, with dwindling food and water, and no sign of any progress since the alarmingly underwhelming texts we saw earlier,” said Rachel Cleetus of the Union of Concerned Scientists, on site. Host Turkey and co-host Australia, which is chairing the negotiations, extended catering and shuttle services into Saturday, and a third draft was promised after overnight consultations. Summit-watchers surveyed by Carbon Brief put the expected finish late on Sunday — more than a day past the deadline, in a summit already being called one of the hardest to close.

Latest · Asia-Pacific · Manipur, India — 11 Dead, 180 in Hospital21 November 2026

Suspected Spurious Liquor Kills 11 and Puts More Than 180 in Hospital in Northeast India

At least 11 people have died and more than 180 have been hospitalised in India’s northeastern state of Manipur after drinking what authorities suspect was adulterated alcohol. The victims, from villages across Chandel district about 62 kilometres from the state capital Imphal, fell ill over the past three days; all of those who died were men aged between 32 and 50. Chief Minister Yumnam Khemchand Singh said more than 180 patients had been admitted to two Imphal hospitals within 48 hours, and visited both facilities on Friday. Police say the liquor was reportedly bought in Sugnu, in neighbouring Kakching district, rather than brewed in Chandel itself, and district officials have issued an advisory urging people to avoid locally brewed liquor and any drink of doubtful origin until further notice, while noting the precise medical cause remains under investigation. Deaths from illicit alcohol are a recurring tragedy in India, where cheap liquor sold outside regulated channels can be laced with methanol, a toxic industrial chemical that can blind or kill in small quantities.

Latest · Americas · Washington — “A Tremendous Tax”20 November 2026

Trump Threatens a “Tremendous Tax” on US Firms That Build AI Data Centres Abroad

President Donald Trump has opened a new front in the artificial-intelligence race: where the data centres get built. Speaking at a White House news conference on Friday after returning from Asia, Trump said American companies would no longer be allowed to build data centres overseas without penalty. “We’re not going to allow our companies to build data centers outside of our country,” he said. “If they do that, they can. But we’re going to put a tremendous tax on them.” He argued that if the vast, power-hungry facilities go abroad, “China will take all of them,” and urged US communities to welcome them instead — “Make them pay you. Make them pay your community” — despite the local backlash over electricity and water use that has pushed some tech giants to look overseas in the first place. The president conceded he had not yet discussed the proposal with his staff, and gave no detail on how such a tax would work or be enforced. The remarks came days after Washington moved to close a loophole that let Chinese firms rent AI computing power from data centres in Southeast Asia and elsewhere running on restricted US hardware; new export controls now require a US licence for facilities anywhere in the world supplying large volumes of advanced-chip computing to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other embargoed territories. The Senate, for its part, approved legislation on 19 November to ease approvals for data centres at home.

Latest · Europe · Paris — 205 Votes to 11020 November 2026

France’s Senate Approves Police Shooting Law That Critics Call a “Licence to Kill”

France’s Senate has given final approval to a law that changes the legal starting point whenever a police officer fires a weapon: the shooting will now be presumed lawful unless there is proof to the contrary. Senators backed the measure by 205 votes to 110 on Friday. The presumption applies even where an officer has shot and killed someone, giving that officer the benefit of the doubt in subsequent proceedings. Supporters, who have sought the change for years and describe it as legal protection tailored to the conditions in which officers must intervene, include politicians of the far right, for whom it has been a long-standing demand. Left-wing parliamentarians oppose the law and say they will challenge it before the Constitutional Council; critics have dubbed it “a licence to kill,” warning that presuming legality could shield misconduct and deepen public distrust of the police. The Council challenge means the law’s final shape may yet be decided by judges rather than legislators.

Latest · Europe · 25 Countries — The Youth Strike20 November 2026

“Fight for Our Future”: Students Walk Out Across 25 European Countries in a Coordinated Youth Strike

A generation that came of age through a pandemic, a cost-of-living squeeze and wars on its continent’s edge took to the streets in unison on Friday. Organisers billed the day as an “international youth strike” across at least 25 countries, with students walking out in Britain, Germany, Spain, Italy, Croatia and beyond. The demands overlapped but varied by city: more funding for schools and universities, action on climate change, relief from high rents and living costs, opposition to rearmament and to any return of military conscription, and anger over the war in Gaza, with Palestinian flags common in the crowds. In Germany, the campaign group “Gen Z regelt” — “Gen Z takes charge” — organised protests in 73 towns and cities; about 4,000 people gathered at Berlin’s Alexanderplatz, where fears of renewed conscription amid rapid rearmament ran through the speeches. In Italy the day fused with a union-backed strike: the CGIL confederation said demonstrators filled more than 100 squares, and its leader Maurizio Landini claimed over a million participants, a figure that could not be independently confirmed. In London, students from more than 350 schools, colleges and universities were expected to walk out toward Parliament Square. The marches were largely peaceful and mostly drew hundreds to thousands per city — and they are not finished: further protests were expected across France and Spain over the weekend, with a transport strike set to disrupt Italian rail lines on Saturday.

Latest · Americas · Washington — The New Congress Takes Shape21 November 2026

The Largest Class of New Black Democrats in 30 Years Is Heading to Congress — With an Ambitious Agenda Behind It

When the new US Congress convenes on 3 January, it will include 13 newly elected Black Democratic House members — the largest such cohort since 1993, according to a Reuters analysis of House historian data — despite Republican-led redistricting that was expected to shrink their numbers. Black representation in the Senate will reach a record seven, following wins including Democrat Juliana Stratton in Illinois and an upset by Mississippi Democrat Scott Colom. Among the newcomers is Melat Kiros, 29, the first Gen Z woman elected to Congress and the first Black woman to represent Colorado. The Congressional Black Caucus will enter the session at a record 64 members. Overall, Black candidates won 58 of the House’s 435 seats, down from 61, with two Louisiana races headed to 12 December runoffs. The arrivals coincide with a Democratic leadership preparing an assertive first-100-days programme after sweeping the 3 November midterms — Democrats will hold the Senate 56–44 and a House majority above 85 votes — with early votes expected on limiting the president’s ability to wage war on Iran without congressional approval, reversing deep cuts to Medicaid and food assistance, and revisiting a federal minimum wage unchanged since 2009. Senator Raphael Warnock, only the 11th Black senator in US history, told Reuters the work of shaping that agenda has to start before January. Whether expanded representation converts into legislative power is the test political observers say now begins.

Latest · Europe · Moscow — Sanctions Reach the Boardroom21 November 2026

Russia’s Biggest Online Retailer Loses Its Chief Executive Days After the EU Sanctioned Her

Sanctions have reached the top of Russia’s consumer internet. Tatyana Kim, founder and head of Wildberries, the country’s largest online retailer, has stepped down, Russian media reported on Saturday, days after the European Union added her to its Russia sanctions list on 16 November. The company has appointed Alexei Kulikov as its new chief executive, according to the TASS and RIA news agencies, citing a corporate filings register. Neither report gave a reason for the change, and Wildberries did not immediately elaborate. The move is a reminder that the EU’s listings reach beyond officials and defence firms into the commanding heights of Russia’s domestic economy: Wildberries is the marketplace through which a large share of Russian households now shop, and its founder was among the country’s most prominent business figures. Her departure, coming within a week of the listing, suggests how quickly a sanctions designation can force a boardroom reckoning — and it lands in the same week that the war itself spilled across the Polish border, keeping the economic and military fronts of the conflict in the same headlines.
